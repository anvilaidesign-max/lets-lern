## What money does

Money is anything widely accepted in payment. It does three jobs:

- **Medium of exchange:** it avoids barter, where you would need to find someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
- **Unit of account:** a common measure for prices, wages and debts.
- **Store of value:** it lets you save today's work to spend later.

Modern money is mostly **fiat money**: paper, coins and bank balances with no value of their own. It works because people trust that others will accept it and that its value will stay reasonably stable. When that trust breaks, money stops doing its jobs, as Zimbabwe experienced.

## Most money is created by banks

Physical cash is a small part of the money supply. Most money is **bank deposits**, and commercial banks create new deposits when they **make loans**. When a bank lends you 1,000, it credits your account with 1,000 of new money; nobody else's balance goes down. Repaying the loan destroys that money again.

Banks cannot create money without limit. They are constrained by the need to make profitable loans, by regulation that requires enough **capital** to absorb losses, by the need to keep enough liquid assets to meet withdrawals, and above all by the **interest rates** set by the central bank.

Because banks lend out most of the deposits they hold, a loss of confidence can trigger a **bank run**: everyone tries to withdraw at once, and even a healthy bank cannot pay. Deposit insurance and central banks acting as **lenders of last resort** exist to prevent this.

## Central banks

A **central bank**, such as the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe, the US Federal Reserve or the South African Reserve Bank, manages the currency and the banking system. Its main tool is the **policy interest rate**:

- **Raising rates** makes borrowing more expensive, cooling spending and investment and bringing inflation down, at the cost of slower growth and possibly higher unemployment.
- **Cutting rates** encourages borrowing and spending, supporting growth and jobs, but risks higher inflation.

Many central banks target low, stable inflation, often around 2–6% a year. **Independence** from politicians matters, because governments are tempted to print money to fund spending before elections.

## What causes inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level, usually measured by a **consumer price index (CPI)** that tracks the cost of a typical basket of goods and services. Its main causes:

- **Demand-pull:** spending grows faster than the economy can produce, so prices rise.
- **Cost-push:** costs such as fuel, imports or wages rise, and firms pass them on. A currency that loses value makes imports dearer.
- **Money growth:** in the long run, if the money supply grows much faster than real output, prices rise. Economist Milton Friedman summed this up as "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon".
- **Expectations:** if everyone expects high inflation, workers demand higher wages and firms raise prices in advance, which makes it self-fulfilling.

Moderate, predictable inflation is manageable. High and unpredictable inflation destroys savings, confuses price signals and discourages investment.

## Zimbabwe's hyperinflation

Zimbabwe experienced one of the worst hyperinflations ever recorded. After economic output collapsed in the early 2000s, the government financed spending by having the central bank print money. Inflation accelerated:

- By July 2008 the official annual rate was about **231 million percent**; independent estimates for November 2008 were vastly higher, with prices doubling roughly **every day**.
- The central bank issued a **100 trillion dollar** note in 2009, and removed many zeros from the currency three times.
- Shops emptied, salaries became worthless within days, and people returned to barter and foreign currency.

In 2009 the country abandoned the Zimbabwe dollar and adopted the **US dollar** and other foreign currencies. Inflation fell almost immediately. Since then, local currencies have returned in several forms: bond notes, the RTGS dollar and Zimbabwe dollar from 2019, and **ZiG** (Zimbabwe Gold), backed by gold and foreign reserves, from April 2024. Each has struggled to win the trust that US dollars enjoy, which is why prices in Zimbabwe are still widely quoted in USD.

The lesson, repeated in Weimar Germany in 1923 and Venezuela in the 2010s: printing money to pay for government spending, when the economy is not producing more, ends in collapse.

## Deflation is also dangerous

**Deflation**, falling prices, sounds pleasant but can trap an economy. People delay purchases because things will be cheaper later, debts become heavier in real terms, and firms cut jobs. Japan struggled with low inflation and deflation for much of the 1990s and 2000s.

## Real and nominal interest rates

The **nominal** interest rate is the stated rate. The **real** interest rate subtracts inflation: a loan at 10% when inflation is 12% has a real rate of about −2%, so borrowers gain and savers lose. When inflation is high and interest rates lag behind, savers are quietly taxed. This is why people in high-inflation economies rush to spend money or convert it into dollars, land, livestock or goods.

# Key points
- Money is a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value; fiat money depends on trust.
- Commercial banks create most money by lending; repaying loans destroys it.
- Central banks raise interest rates to fight inflation and cut them to support growth.
- Inflation comes from excess demand, rising costs, rapid money growth and expectations.
- Zimbabwe's hyperinflation, driven by money printing, ended with dollarisation in 2009.
- Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation.
