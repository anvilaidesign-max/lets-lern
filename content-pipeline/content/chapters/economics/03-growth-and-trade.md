## Measuring an economy: GDP

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year. It can be measured three ways that should give the same answer: total **spending**, total **output**, or total **income** earned. The spending approach is the best known:

> GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

- **C:** household consumption.
- **I:** investment by firms, such as factories, machines and new buildings.
- **G:** government spending on goods and services.
- **X − M:** exports minus imports (**net exports**).

Only **final** goods count. The steel in a car is counted once, as part of the car's price, not again on its own.

## Real, per person and PPP

Raw GDP numbers mislead unless adjusted:

- **Real GDP** removes inflation. If nominal GDP rises 10% but prices rose 8%, the economy grew only about 2%.
- **GDP per capita** divides by population. A country whose GDP grows 3% while its population grows 3% is not getting richer on average.
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** adjusts for price differences between countries. A haircut costs far less in Harare than in London, so the same dollar income buys more locally.

## What GDP misses

GDP ignores unpaid work at home, most of the **informal economy** (large in Zimbabwe, where many people work in informal trade), environmental damage, and how income is **distributed**. A country can grow while most people see no improvement. Economists therefore also track poverty rates, the **Gini coefficient** for inequality, and the **Human Development Index**, which combines income, education and life expectancy.

## What makes economies grow

In the long run, living standards rise when each worker produces more, which is **productivity**. It grows through:

- **Physical capital:** machines, roads, power grids, telecoms.
- **Human capital:** education, skills and health.
- **Technology and ideas:** better ways of producing things. This is the main source of sustained growth in modern economies.
- **Institutions:** secure property rights, rule of law, low corruption and stable policy, which make people willing to invest for the long term.

**Reliable electricity** is a striking example: factories and businesses that face load-shedding lose output and must buy generators, raising costs across the whole economy. Infrastructure is where engineering and economics meet.

## Comparative advantage

Why trade at all? In 1817 David Ricardo showed something surprising. Even if one country is better at producing **everything**, both countries gain if each specialises in what it is **relatively** best at, its **comparative advantage**, and trades.

> Example: in one day, country A can make 10 tonnes of maize or 5 machines; country B can make 6 tonnes of maize or 1 machine. A is better at both. But making one machine costs A only 2 tonnes of maize, while it costs B 6 tonnes. So A should specialise in machines and B in maize. After trading, both can have more of both goods than if each made everything itself.

Trade also brings bigger markets, competition and access to technology. It creates losers too: workers in industries that can't compete with imports. Good policy helps them retrain and move, rather than blocking trade altogether.

## Exchange rates and the trade balance

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in another. When a currency **weakens**, exports become cheaper for foreigners and imports become dearer at home. A **trade deficit** means a country imports more than it exports and must finance the gap by borrowing, attracting investment or receiving transfers such as **remittances**. Remittances from Zimbabweans working abroad are a major source of foreign currency for the country.

A **current account** deficit is not automatically bad, since it can finance productive investment, but large, persistent deficits funded by short-term borrowing are risky.

## Zimbabwe's economy and the resource curse

Zimbabwe's main exports are **gold**, **platinum group metals**, **tobacco**, **nickel**, **diamonds** and, increasingly, **lithium**, where Zimbabwe has become Africa's largest producer. Agriculture, mining and services such as tourism around Victoria Falls employ large numbers of people.

Natural resources can fund development, but many resource-rich countries grow more slowly than resource-poor ones, a pattern called the **resource curse**. The causes:

- Commodity **price swings** make government revenue volatile.
- Mineral exports can strengthen the currency and hurt manufacturing and farming (**Dutch disease**).
- Resource wealth can encourage **corruption** and conflict over who controls it.

Countries that escape it, such as Botswana with its diamonds, tend to have strong institutions, transparent contracts, sovereign wealth funds and policies that add value locally, such as processing lithium into battery materials rather than exporting raw ore.

# Key points
- GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) measures a country's output in a year.
- Use real GDP for growth, per capita for living standards and PPP for comparisons.
- GDP misses informal work, inequality and environmental damage.
- Long-run growth comes from productivity: capital, skills, technology and good institutions.
- Comparative advantage means both countries can gain from trade, even if one is better at everything.
- Resource wealth can become a curse without strong institutions.
