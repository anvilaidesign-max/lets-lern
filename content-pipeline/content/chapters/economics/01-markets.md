## Scarcity and choice

Economics studies how people, businesses and governments use **scarce** resources. There is never enough time, money, land, labour or energy to do everything, so every choice has a cost.

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up. The cost of spending four years at university is not just fees; it includes the income you could have earned working. A government that spends a billion on one project cannot spend it on hospitals. Thinking in opportunity costs is the most useful habit economics teaches.

## Demand

**Demand** describes how much of something buyers want at each price. The **law of demand**: when the price rises, the quantity demanded usually falls, because people buy less, switch to substitutes, or can't afford as much.

Plotted with price on the vertical axis, the **demand curve** slopes downwards. Things other than price **shift** the whole curve:

- **Income:** richer customers buy more of most goods (normal goods).
- **Prices of related goods:** if bread gets expensive, demand for rice (a **substitute**) rises; if cars get expensive, demand for petrol (a **complement**) falls.
- **Tastes, expectations and population.** Expecting prices to rise next month makes people buy now.

## Supply

**Supply** describes how much sellers will offer at each price. The **law of supply**: higher prices encourage more production, because it becomes more profitable, so the supply curve slopes upwards. Supply shifts with production costs, technology, the number of sellers, weather (for crops) and taxes.

## Equilibrium

The market settles where the curves cross: the **equilibrium** price, where the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer.

- If the price is **above** equilibrium, there is a **surplus**: unsold stock pushes prices down.
- If the price is **below** equilibrium, there is a **shortage**: queues and competition push prices up.

No one needs to coordinate this. Prices carry information: a rising price tells producers "make more of this" and buyers "use less of this". Adam Smith called this the **invisible hand**.

## Shifts versus movements

A crucial distinction:

- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the curve.
- A change in **anything else** **shifts** the curve.

> Example: a drought cuts the maize harvest. Supply shifts left, so the price rises and the quantity sold falls. Buyers move along the demand curve, but demand itself has not changed.

## Elasticity

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how strongly quantity responds to price:

> Elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

- **Elastic** (greater than 1 in size): quantity responds strongly. Goods with close substitutes, such as a particular brand of drink, are elastic.
- **Inelastic** (less than 1): quantity barely changes. Necessities with no substitutes, such as fuel, basic food and medicine, are inelastic in the short run.

This matters in practice. Raising the price of an inelastic good raises total revenue, which is why governments tax fuel, alcohol and cigarettes. Raising the price of an elastic good can cut revenue as customers leave.

## When governments fix prices

- A **price ceiling** below equilibrium, meant to keep goods affordable, creates **shortages**. Producers make less, buyers want more, and goods disappear into queues and black markets. In 2007 Zimbabwe ordered shops to cut prices sharply during high inflation, and shelves emptied within weeks.
- A **price floor** above equilibrium creates **surpluses**. A minimum wage set well above market wages can reduce jobs for low-skilled workers, though modest minimum wages often show small effects in research.

Controls treat the symptom, the price, not the cause, such as scarcity or money printing.

## Market failure

Markets work well under certain conditions. They fail when:

- **Externalities:** costs or benefits fall on people outside the deal. A factory polluting a river imposes costs on people downstream that the factory does not pay, so it produces too much. Vaccinations benefit others too, so people get too few without support.
- **Public goods:** things like street lighting and national defence, which people can use without paying, so private markets underprovide them.
- **Monopoly power:** a single seller can raise prices and restrict output. Competition law exists to prevent this.
- **Information asymmetry:** one side knows more, such as a seller of a used car with hidden faults, which can make markets break down.

Government policy, such as taxes on pollution, public provision, regulation and consumer protection, aims to correct these failures. Governments can fail too, so good policy weighs both.

# Key points
- Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up.
- Demand slopes down, supply slopes up; equilibrium is where they cross.
- A change in the good's own price moves along a curve; any other change shifts it.
- Elasticity measures how much quantity responds to price; necessities are inelastic.
- Price ceilings below equilibrium cause shortages; price floors above it cause surpluses.
- Markets fail with externalities, public goods, monopoly and information asymmetry.
