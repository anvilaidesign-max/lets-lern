## What an argument is

In everyday speech, an argument is a quarrel. In writing and debate, it is a **reasoned case** for a position. Every argument has three parts:

- A **claim:** what you want the reader to accept. *Zimbabwe should invest in rooftop solar for schools.*
- **Evidence:** facts, data, examples, expert findings. *Load-shedding cut many schools' power for long periods; solar costs have fallen sharply over the past decade.*
- **Reasoning** (the warrant): why the evidence supports the claim. *Reliable power is needed for computers and lighting, and solar now provides it more cheaply than diesel generators.*

The philosopher Stephen Toulmin added **qualifiers** (*in most cases*) and **rebuttals** (*unless batteries are stolen*). Strong arguments admit their limits.

## Ethos, pathos and logos

More than 2,300 years ago, Aristotle described three ways to persuade an audience:

- **Ethos:** credibility. The audience trusts the speaker because of expertise, experience or character. *As an engineer who has installed 200 systems...*
- **Pathos:** emotion. Stories and images that make the audience feel something. *Imagine a nurse delivering a baby by the light of a phone torch.*
- **Logos:** logic and evidence. Numbers, reasoning and examples. *Each outage costs the factory 12% of daily output.*

The most persuasive messages combine all three. Pure logic can feel cold; pure emotion can feel manipulative; credibility without evidence is just an appeal to authority.

## Structuring an argumentative essay

- **Introduction:** hook the reader, give essential context, and end with a clear **thesis statement**, your answer to the question in one or two sentences.
- **Body paragraphs:** each develops one reason supporting the thesis, using PEEL. Order them logically, often strongest first or last.
- **Counterargument and rebuttal:** fairly present the strongest opposing view, then show why your position still holds.
- **Conclusion:** restate the thesis in fresh words, summarise the key reasons, and end with implications or a call to action. Do not add new evidence.

A thesis must be **arguable**. *Electricity is important* is not a thesis; nobody disagrees. *Zimbabwe should require solar water heaters on all new buildings* is.

## Handling the other side

Addressing counterarguments makes your case stronger, not weaker. It shows you have thought carefully, and it answers the reader's doubts before they grow. The most effective approach is the **steelman**: present the opposing view in its strongest form, not a weak version that is easy to knock down.

> Example: *Critics argue that solar is too expensive upfront for schools. That is a real barrier. But pay-as-you-go financing and donor grants spread the cost, and savings on diesel repay the investment within a few years.*

## Logical fallacies

A **fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument look stronger than it is. Learn to spot them in others' arguments and avoid them in your own:

- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person instead of the argument. *You can't trust his plan; he failed maths at school.*
- **Straw man:** misrepresenting an opponent's view to make it easier to attack.
- **False dilemma:** presenting only two options when more exist. *Either we ban phones in class or students will never learn.*
- **Slippery slope:** claiming one step will inevitably lead to extreme outcomes, without evidence.
- **Appeal to authority:** treating something as true just because a famous or powerful person said it, especially outside their expertise.
- **Hasty generalisation:** drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples. *My cousin's solar panel broke, so solar doesn't work.*
- **Post hoc** (false cause): assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
- **Bandwagon:** *Everyone is investing in this scheme, so it must be good.*

Scams, propaganda and advertising rely heavily on these. Spotting them is a form of self-defence.

## Reading critically

When you read an argument, ask:

- What exactly is the claim?
- What evidence is offered, and is it reliable, recent and relevant?
- Who is making the argument, and what do they gain?
- What is left out? What would the other side say?
- Does the conclusion actually follow from the evidence?

## Speaking and presenting

Persuasion in speech follows the same principles, with a few additions:

- **Know your one message.** If the audience remembers one sentence, what should it be? Say it early and repeat it at the end.
- **Structure:** tell them what you will say, say it, then tell them what you said.
- **Slides support, not replace, you.** Use few words, large figures and one idea per slide. Never read from slides.
- **Stories and examples** stick far better than abstract statements.
- **Delivery:** slow down, pause after key points, make eye contact, and stand steadily. Nervousness is normal; practising aloud several times is the best cure.
- **Handling questions:** listen fully, repeat the question if needed, answer briefly, and admit when you don't know: *Good question; I'll check and get back to you.*

# Key points
- An argument has a claim, evidence and reasoning; strong ones include qualifiers and rebuttals.
- Aristotle's modes: ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), logos (logic).
- A good thesis is clear and arguable; body paragraphs each give one reason.
- Address the strongest counterargument (steelman), then rebut it.
- Common fallacies: ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, hasty generalisation, post hoc.
- In presentations, have one clear message and support it with stories and evidence.
