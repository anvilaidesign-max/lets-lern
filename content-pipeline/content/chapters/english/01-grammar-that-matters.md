## Why grammar still matters

Grammar is not about sounding posh. In reports, emails, CVs and exams, errors make readers work harder and trust you less. A job application with "their" instead of "there" can be rejected in seconds. The good news: a small number of rules cause most mistakes.

## The building blocks

Every complete sentence needs a **subject** (who or what) and a **verb** (what happens). Most also have an **object** (what the action affects):

- *The engineer* (subject) *tested* (verb) *the circuit* (object).

A **clause** is a group of words with its own subject and verb.

- A **main (independent) clause** can stand alone: *The motor stopped.*
- A **dependent (subordinate) clause** cannot: *When the fuse blew...* leaves the reader waiting. It starts with a word like **when, because, although, if, which** or **that**.

Sentences combine clauses in three ways:

- **Simple:** one main clause.
- **Compound:** two main clauses joined by **and, but, or, so** (with a comma) or by a semicolon.
- **Complex:** a main clause plus one or more dependent clauses.

Mixing the three gives writing rhythm. Too many simple sentences sound choppy; too many long complex ones exhaust the reader.

## Subject-verb agreement

The verb must match its subject in number:

- The result **is** clear. / The results **are** clear.
- Watch for words between the subject and verb: The list of components **is** long (the subject is *list*, not *components*).
- *Each*, *every*, *everyone* and *nobody* take a singular verb: Everyone **has** a copy.
- *Data* is technically plural, but "the data **shows**" is now widely accepted in general writing; scientific journals often prefer "the data **show**". Pick one and be consistent.

## Tenses that trip people up

- **Past simple** for finished events at a known time: I **finished** the report yesterday.
- **Present perfect** (*have/has* + past participle) for past actions connected to now, with no specific time: I **have finished** the report (it is ready now). You cannot say ~~I have finished it yesterday~~.
- **Present continuous** for things happening now or planned: I **am testing** the board this afternoon.
- In reports, describe your method in the **past** (*We measured the voltage*) and established facts in the **present** (*Ohm's law states that...*).

## Commonly confused words

- **its** (belonging to it) / **it's** (it is): *It's clear the machine lost its power.*
- **their** (belonging to them) / **there** (a place) / **they're** (they are).
- **your** / **you're** (you are).
- **affect** (usually a verb: to influence) / **effect** (usually a noun: a result). *Heat affects resistance; the effect is measurable.*
- **fewer** (things you can count) / **less** (amounts): *fewer errors, less noise*.
- **then** (time) / **than** (comparison).
- **principle** (a rule) / **principal** (main, or a school head).
- **complement** (completes) / **compliment** (praise).
- **lose** (misplace) / **loose** (not tight).

## Punctuation that matters

- **Comma splice:** joining two main clauses with only a comma is an error. ~~The test failed, we changed the resistor.~~ Fix it with a full stop, a semicolon, or a joining word: *The test failed, so we changed the resistor.*
- **Semicolon (;)** joins two closely related main clauses: *The first design overheated; the second ran cool.*
- **Colon (:)** introduces a list, an explanation or an example: *We need three things: time, money and data.*
- **Apostrophes** show possession (*the engineer's report*, *the engineers' reports*) or missing letters (*don't*). They never make ordinary plurals: ~~laptop's for sale~~.
- Commas around extra information: *The inverter, which was new, failed after a week.* Without commas, *which/that* restricts the meaning: *The inverter that failed was new.*

## Active and passive voice

In the **active voice**, the subject does the action: *The technician replaced the fuse.* In the **passive voice**, the subject receives it: *The fuse was replaced (by the technician).*

Active voice is usually clearer, shorter and more direct, so prefer it in most writing. The passive is useful when the doer is unknown or unimportant, which is why it is common in lab reports: *The sample was heated to 80 °C.* Many modern scientific journals now accept "we" in the active voice too: *We heated the sample to 80 °C.*

## Dangling modifiers

A descriptive phrase must describe the word right next to it:

- ~~Walking into the lab, the smell of burning plastic was obvious.~~ (The smell was not walking.)
- Walking into the lab, **I** noticed the smell of burning plastic.

# Key points
- A sentence needs a subject and a verb; a dependent clause cannot stand alone.
- Verbs agree with the true subject, not nearby nouns; each and everyone are singular.
- Use the present perfect for past actions linked to now, and the past simple with a specific time.
- Master the confusables: its/it's, their/there/they're, affect/effect, fewer/less.
- Never join two main clauses with just a comma; use a full stop, semicolon or conjunction.
- Prefer the active voice; use the passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant.

# Quiz
Q: Which sentence is correct?
- The list of results are on the table.
* The list of results is on the table.
- The list of results be on the table.
- The list of results were on the table.
> The subject is "list" (singular), so the verb is "is".

Q: Which sentence uses the present perfect correctly?
- I have finished the report yesterday.
* I have finished the report, so you can read it now.
- I have finish the report.
- I finished the report now already.
> The present perfect links a past action to now and is not used with a specific past time like "yesterday".

Q: What is wrong with "The test failed, we changed the resistor."?
- Nothing
- It uses the passive voice
* It is a comma splice: two main clauses joined only by a comma
- It has no verb
> Use a full stop, semicolon or a joining word such as "so".

Q: Choose the correct sentence.
* It's clear the machine lost its power.
- Its clear the machine lost it's power.
- It's clear the machine lost it's power.
- Its clear the machine lost its power.
> "It's" means "it is"; "its" shows possession.

Q: When is the passive voice most useful?
- In every sentence, to sound formal
* When the doer of the action is unknown or unimportant
- Only in questions
- Never, it is always wrong
> The passive focuses on the action or result, as in "The sample was heated to 80 °C."
