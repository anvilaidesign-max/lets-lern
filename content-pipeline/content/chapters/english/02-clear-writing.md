## Start with the reader

Before writing anything, answer three questions:

- **Who** will read this? A lecturer, a client, a manager, a technician? What do they already know?
- **Why** are you writing? To inform, to request, to persuade, to record?
- **What** should the reader know or do after reading?

Clear writing is about the reader's time. A busy manager should be able to act on your email in thirty seconds. An examiner should find your argument without hunting for it.

## Plain English

**Plain language** means writing so your audience can understand it the first time. Governments and companies around the world have adopted it because confusing documents waste time and cause mistakes.

- **Short words:** *use*, not *utilise*; *start*, not *commence*; *help*, not *facilitate*.
- **Short sentences:** aim for an average of 15 to 20 words. Split long ones.
- **Active voice:** *The contractor will install the panels*, not *The panels will be installed by the contractor*.
- **Concrete words:** *The pump failed after 40 hours*, not *There were performance issues*.
- **Cut filler:** *in order to* → *to*; *due to the fact that* → *because*; *at this point in time* → *now*; *it should be noted that* → delete it.

Technical terms are fine when your reader knows them. Define them the first time for a general reader.

## Main point first

In school essays, the conclusion often comes at the end. In professional writing, put the most important information **first**, a principle the military calls **BLUF: bottom line up front**.

- Weak: *Following our meeting last week, I looked into several options, considered costs, and after some thought...*
- Strong: *I recommend we buy the 5 kVA inverter. It costs 20% less over five years. Details below.*

The reader can stop after the first line and still know what matters.

## Paragraphs with PEEL

Each paragraph should develop **one idea**. The **PEEL** structure keeps paragraphs focused, especially in essays and reports:

- **Point:** a topic sentence stating the paragraph's main idea.
- **Evidence:** facts, data, examples or a cited source.
- **Explain:** how the evidence supports the point. This is where most marks are won or lost.
- **Link:** connect back to the overall argument or lead into the next paragraph.

## Professional emails

- **Subject line:** specific and useful. *Invoice 214 overdue: payment by Friday?*, not *Hello*.
- **Greeting:** *Dear Ms Moyo* for formal first contact; *Hi Tendai* once you know them.
- **First line:** why you are writing or what you need.
- **Body:** short paragraphs; bullet points for several items or questions.
- **Clear action:** what you want, and by when.
- **Sign-off:** *Kind regards* or *Best regards*, your name and, if useful, your role and phone number.

Before sending, check names, attachments and tone. Emails are permanent, and anger reads worse in writing: if you are upset, wait an hour.

## Technical and lab reports

Most scientific and engineering reports follow a standard structure (often called **IMRAD**):

- **Title and abstract:** a short summary of the purpose, method, key results and conclusion, often 150–250 words. Many readers read only this.
- **Introduction:** the problem, background and objectives.
- **Method:** what you did, in enough detail for someone else to repeat it.
- **Results:** what you found, with tables and labelled graphs (units on every axis). Report without interpreting.
- **Discussion:** what the results mean, sources of error, and comparison with theory.
- **Conclusion and recommendations:** answer the objectives directly.
- **References** and **appendices** (raw data, long calculations, code).

Number figures and tables and refer to them in the text: *As Figure 3 shows, efficiency peaked at 60% load.*

## Using sources honestly

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, and universities treat it as serious misconduct. Avoid it by:

- putting direct quotes in quotation marks, with a citation;
- **paraphrasing** properly: rewriting the idea in your own words and structure, still with a citation;
- citing data, images, diagrams and code, not just text; and
- using your institution's referencing style consistently, such as **Harvard** (*author, year*) or **IEEE** (numbered references, common in engineering).

AI tools can help you brainstorm or check grammar, but submitting AI-generated text as your own work breaks most universities' rules. Check your institution's policy.

## Editing

First drafts are for getting ideas down; editing makes them clear. A practical checklist:

- Is the main point in the first paragraph?
- Does each paragraph have one clear idea?
- Can any sentence be cut or shortened?
- Are numbers, units, names and dates correct?
- Read it **aloud**: you will hear clumsy sentences that your eyes skip over.
- If possible, leave it overnight and read it fresh.

# Key points
- Know who your reader is, why you are writing, and what they should do next.
- Plain English: short words and sentences, active voice, concrete detail, no filler.
- Put the bottom line up front in professional writing.
- PEEL paragraphs: point, evidence, explain, link.
- Reports follow a standard structure: abstract, introduction, method, results, discussion, conclusion.
- Cite all sources; paraphrasing without citation is still plagiarism.
