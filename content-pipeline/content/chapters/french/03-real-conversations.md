## Politeness comes first

In French-speaking cultures, politeness rules matter more than perfect grammar:

- **Always greet before anything else.** Say *Bonjour* (or *Bonsoir* in the evening) when entering a shop, office or taxi, before asking a question. Skipping it is seen as rude.
- Add a title: *Bonjour madame*, *Bonjour monsieur*.
- Use **s'il vous plaît** (please), **merci** (thank you) and **pardon** or **excusez-moi** (excuse me) generously.
- Leave with *Au revoir*, *Bonne journée* (have a good day) or *Bonne soirée* (have a good evening).
- Use **vous** with anyone you don't know well.

## Introducing yourself

- *Je m'appelle Chipo. Je suis zimbabwéenne.* (I'm called Chipo. I'm Zimbabwean.) A man would say *zimbabwéen*.
- *J'habite à Harare.* (I live in Harare.)
- *Je suis étudiant en génie électronique.* (I'm an electronic engineering student.) A woman says *étudiante*.
- *Je travaille dans l'énergie solaire.* (I work in solar energy.)
- *Je parle anglais, shona et un peu français.* (I speak English, Shona and a little French.)
- *Enchanté(e) !* (Pleased to meet you!)

Useful replies when you don't understand:

- *Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?* (Could you repeat, please?)
- *Pouvez-vous parler plus lentement ?* (Could you speak more slowly?)
- *Comment dit-on « charger » en anglais ?* (How do you say ... in English?)
- *Je ne comprends pas.* (I don't understand.)

## At the café, restaurant and market

- *Je voudrais...* (I would like...) is the key phrase for ordering.
- *Qu'est-ce que vous me conseillez ?* (What do you recommend?)
- *C'est combien ?* or *Ça coûte combien ?* (How much is it?)
- *L'addition, s'il vous plaît.* (The bill, please.)
- *Je peux payer par carte / par mobile money ?* (Can I pay by card / mobile money?)
- *C'est trop cher. Vous pouvez faire un prix ?* (It's too expensive. Can you give me a better price?) is common in West African markets.

## Numbers that surprise learners

1 to 60 are regular: *un, deux, trois... dix, onze, douze... vingt, trente, quarante, cinquante, soixante*. Then French in France counts in twenties:

- **70** = *soixante-dix* (sixty-ten); 75 = *soixante-quinze*
- **80** = *quatre-vingts* (four twenties)
- **90** = *quatre-vingt-dix* (four twenties ten); 97 = *quatre-vingt-dix-sept*
- **100** = *cent*; **1,000** = *mille*; **1,000,000** = *un million*

Belgium and Switzerland use simpler words: *septante* (70), *nonante* (90), and in parts of Switzerland *huitante* (80). French also writes numbers differently: **1 000 000,50** means one million point five zero. The comma is the decimal point, and spaces separate thousands. This matters when reading technical documents and invoices.

## Getting around

- *Où se trouve la gare / l'hôpital / l'université ?* (Where is the station / hospital / university?)
- *C'est loin ?* (Is it far?)
- *Tout droit* (straight on), *à gauche* (left), *à droite* (right), *au coin* (at the corner), *à côté de* (next to), *en face de* (opposite).
- *Je voudrais un billet pour Dakar.* (I'd like a ticket to Dakar.)

## Professional French

French business writing is more formal than English.

**Emails:**

- Opening: *Madame, Monsieur,* (to someone unknown), or *Bonjour Madame Diallo,*.
- *Je me permets de vous contacter au sujet de...* (I'm writing to you about...)
- *Vous trouverez ci-joint mon CV.* (Please find my CV attached.)
- *Je reste à votre disposition pour tout renseignement complémentaire.* (I'm available for any further information.)
- Closing: *Cordialement* (kind regards) for most emails. In formal letters: *Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*

**On the phone:**

- *Allô, bonjour, je suis bien chez Solaris ?* (Hello, is this Solaris?)
- *Pourrais-je parler à M. Kaboré, s'il vous plaît ?* (Could I speak to Mr Kaboré, please?)
- *C'est de la part de qui ?* (Who's calling?)
- *Je rappellerai plus tard.* (I'll call back later.)

## False friends

Some French words look like English words but mean something else:

- *actuellement* = **currently** (not actually)
- *éventuellement* = **possibly** (not eventually)
- *librairie* = **bookshop** (library = *bibliothèque*)
- *attendre* = **to wait** (to attend = *assister à*)
- *sensible* = **sensitive** (sensible = *raisonnable*)
- *blesser* = **to injure** (to bless = *bénir*)
- *une journée* = **a day** (a journey = *un voyage*)
- *un stage* = **an internship**
- *la monnaie* = **change, coins** (money in general = *l'argent*)

## African French

French in Africa has its own vocabulary, rhythm and expressions, shaped by local languages. You will hear *une essencerie* (a petrol station) in Senegal, *un maquis* (a small open-air restaurant) in Côte d'Ivoire, and *on est ensemble* (we're together, a friendly sign-off) across West Africa. Nouchi in Abidjan and Camfranglais in Cameroon mix French with local languages and English. Standard French is understood everywhere, but learning local expressions wins friends quickly.

## How to keep improving

- **Little and often:** 15 minutes a day beats three hours once a week.
- **Listen:** French-language radio and podcasts, such as RFI (Radio France Internationale), which has a large African audience, and its slow-news service *Journal en français facile*.
- **Watch** films and series with French subtitles.
- **Speak from day one**, even badly. Find a language partner online or at an Alliance Française centre; Harare and Bulawayo both have one.
- **Learn phrases, not just words:** *je voudrais*, *est-ce que je peux*, *il faut que*.

# Key points
- Always greet with Bonjour madame/monsieur before asking anything.
- Key phrases: je voudrais, pouvez-vous répéter, c'est combien, l'addition s'il vous plaît.
- 70 = soixante-dix, 80 = quatre-vingts, 90 = quatre-vingt-dix; Belgium and Switzerland use septante and nonante.
- French uses a comma as the decimal point and spaces between thousands.
- Business French is formal: Madame, Monsieur and Cordialement.
- Watch false friends: actuellement means currently, librairie means bookshop.
