## Why French is worth it

French is an official language in 29 countries and is spoken on every continent. **Most French speakers now live in Africa**, in countries such as the DRC, Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Cameroon and Madagascar, and the share is growing. For anyone working in business, engineering, diplomacy or development across Africa, French opens half the continent. It is also a working language of the AU, the UN and many international organisations.

## How French sounds

French spelling looks tricky, but it follows patterns:

- **Final consonants are usually silent:** *petit* (puh-TEE), *vous* (voo), *les* (lay). A useful exception rule: final **c, r, f, l** are often pronounced (think of the word *CaReFuL*): *avec*, *pour*, *neuf*, *hôtel*. Final *-er* in verbs is not an exception: *parler* ends in "ay".
- **Final -e is silent** but makes the consonant before it heard: *petit* (puh-TEE) vs *petite* (puh-TEET).
- **The h is always silent:** *hôtel* (oh-TEL).
- **Nasal vowels:** *an/en* (as in *enfant*), *on* (*bon*), *in/ain* (*vin*, *pain*), *un*. The vowel goes partly through the nose and the n is not fully pronounced.
- **Liaison:** a normally silent final consonant is pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel: *les amis* (lay-za-MEE), *vous avez* (voo-za-VAY).
- The French **r** is made at the back of the throat, a little like a soft gargle.

## Accents

Accents change pronunciation or distinguish words:

- **é** (accent aigu): "ay", as in *café*, *été*.
- **è** (accent grave): open "eh", as in *mère*; on *à* and *où*, it distinguishes words (*a* = has, *à* = to).
- **ê** (circonflexe): often marks an **s** that disappeared from old French: *forêt* (forest), *hôpital* (hospital).
- **ç** (cédille): makes c sound like s before a, o, u: *français*, *ça*.
- **ë, ï** (tréma): pronounce the vowels separately: *Noël*.

## Every noun has a gender

All French nouns are **masculine** or **feminine**, even objects. Always learn a noun **with its article**: not *table* but *la table*. Some helpful patterns:

- Usually feminine: nouns ending in **-tion, -sion** (*la nation*), **-té** (*la société*), **-ure** (*la voiture*), **-ence/-ance** (*la science*).
- Usually masculine: nouns ending in **-ment** (*le gouvernement*), **-age** (*le fromage*), **-isme** (*le tourisme*), and most ending in a consonant.

## Articles

Articles change with gender and number:

- **Definite** (the): *le* (m), *la* (f), *l'* (before a vowel or silent h), *les* (plural).
- **Indefinite** (a, some): *un* (m), *une* (f), *des* (plural).
- **Partitive** (some, an unspecified amount): *du* (m), *de la* (f), *de l'*, *des*. *Je bois du café* (I drink [some] coffee).

French uses articles more than English. *J'aime le café* means "I like coffee" (in general). After a negation, *un*, *une*, *du*, *de la* and *des* usually become **de**: *Je n'ai pas de voiture* (I don't have a car).

Some prepositions merge with *le* and *les*: **à + le = au**, **à + les = aux**, **de + le = du**, **de + les = des**. *Je vais au marché*; *le prix du pain*.

## Adjectives agree

Adjectives match their noun in gender and number, usually by adding **-e** (feminine) and **-s** (plural): *un grand bâtiment*, *une grande maison*, *des grandes maisons*.

Most adjectives come **after** the noun: *une voiture rouge*, *un ingénieur compétent*. A small group comes **before**, remembered as **BANGS**: **B**eauty (*beau, joli*), **A**ge (*jeune, vieux, nouveau*), **N**umber (*premier, deux*), **G**oodness (*bon, mauvais*), **S**ize (*grand, petit*): *un bon ami*, *une petite ville*.

## Tu or vous?

French has two words for "you":

- **Tu:** one person you know well: family, friends, children, classmates.
- **Vous:** one person politely (strangers, older people, clients, teachers, officials), **and** any group of people.

When in doubt, use **vous**. Using *tu* too soon can seem rude; the other person will often suggest *on peut se tutoyer* (we can use *tu*).

## The two most important verbs

**Être** (to be): *je suis, tu es, il/elle est, nous sommes, vous êtes, ils/elles sont*.

**Avoir** (to have): *j'ai, tu as, il/elle a, nous avons, vous avez, ils/elles ont*.

French uses *avoir* where English uses "to be" in some expressions: *J'ai 22 ans* (I am 22), *j'ai faim* (I'm hungry), *j'ai froid* (I'm cold), *j'ai raison* (I'm right).

## Saying no

Wrap the verb with **ne ... pas**: Je **ne** parle **pas** allemand (I don't speak German). Before a vowel, *ne* becomes *n'*: Je **n'**ai **pas** le temps (I don't have time). Other negatives follow the same pattern: *ne ... jamais* (never), *ne ... plus* (no longer), *ne ... rien* (nothing). In casual speech the *ne* is often dropped: *Je sais pas.*

## Asking questions

Three ways, from casual to formal:

- **Intonation:** *Tu parles français ?* (voice rises at the end).
- **Est-ce que:** *Est-ce que tu parles français ?* Works for everything and is always correct.
- **Inversion:** *Parlez-vous français ?* Formal and common in writing.

Question words: *qui* (who), *que/quoi* (what), *où* (where), *quand* (when), *pourquoi* (why), *comment* (how), *combien* (how much). *Où est la gare ?* *Combien ça coûte ?*

# Key points
- Most French speakers live in Africa, and French is a working language of the AU and UN.
- Final consonants are usually silent, except often c, r, f and l; liaison links words before vowels.
- Every noun is masculine or feminine; learn each noun with its article.
- Articles: le/la/les, un/une/des, du/de la; à + le = au, de + le = du.
- Most adjectives follow the noun; BANGS adjectives come before it.
- Use vous for politeness and groups; negation wraps the verb in ne ... pas.

# Quiz
Q: Which article goes with "voiture" (car), a feminine noun?
- le
* la
- les
- du
> Feminine singular nouns take la (or une).

Q: What is the correct way to say "I am 20 years old" in French?
- Je suis 20 ans
* J'ai 20 ans
- Je fais 20 ans
- J'ai 20 années vieux
> French uses avoir (to have) for age.

Q: You are meeting a company director for the first time. Which form of "you" should you use?
- Tu
* Vous
- Toi
- Either, it makes no difference
> Vous is the polite form for strangers, officials and professional contacts.

Q: How do you say "I don't have a car"?
- Je n'ai pas une voiture
* Je n'ai pas de voiture
- Je ne pas ai voiture
- Je n'ai voiture pas
> After a negation, un and une usually become de.

Q: What is "à + le" in French?
- à le
* au
- du
- aux
> à + le contracts to au: Je vais au marché.
