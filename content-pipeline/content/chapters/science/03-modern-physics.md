## When Newton is not enough

By 1900 physics seemed nearly complete, yet a few experiments did not fit. Explaining them produced the two great theories of modern physics: **relativity**, for the very fast and the very massive, and **quantum mechanics**, for the very small. Without them there would be no GPS, no nuclear power, no transistors and no lasers.

## Special relativity

In 1905 Albert Einstein built special relativity on two ideas: the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving at constant velocity, and the speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all observers. The consequences are strange but measured again and again:

- **Time dilation:** moving clocks run slow. Muons created by cosmic rays high in the atmosphere should decay long before reaching the ground, yet many arrive, because from our view their internal clocks are slowed.
- **Length contraction:** fast-moving objects are shorter in their direction of motion.
- **A speed limit:** nothing with mass can reach the speed of light, because its energy would become infinite.

## E = mc²

Special relativity also showed that mass and energy are equivalent:

> E = m × c²

Because c² is huge (about 9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²), a tiny amount of mass holds enormous energy: 1 gram corresponds to about 9 × 10¹³ joules. In nuclear fission, as in reactors, a heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 splits and the products weigh very slightly less than the original; that missing mass is released as energy. The Sun shines by **fusion**, joining hydrogen nuclei into helium and converting about 4 million tonnes of mass into energy every second.

## General relativity and GPS

In 1915 Einstein extended the theory to gravity: mass curves spacetime, and objects follow that curvature. Clocks run slower in stronger gravity. **GPS** satellites must correct for both effects. Their speed makes their clocks run slow by about 7 microseconds a day; the weaker gravity at their altitude makes them run fast by about 45 microseconds a day. Without correcting the net 38 microseconds, positions would drift by around 10 km per day.

## Light as particles

The **photoelectric effect** showed that light shining on a metal ejects electrons only if its frequency is high enough, no matter how bright it is. Einstein explained this in 1905: light comes in packets, **photons**, each carrying energy:

> E = h × f      (h ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, Planck's constant)

A single blue photon has more energy than a red one. This work earned Einstein the 1921 Nobel Prize, and it is the principle behind solar cells and camera sensors.

## Waves, particles and uncertainty

Electrons, like light, behave as both waves and particles. Quantum mechanics describes them with probabilities rather than exact paths:

- **Superposition:** a quantum system can be in a combination of states until it is measured. Quantum computers use this in their qubits.
- **Heisenberg's uncertainty principle:** the more precisely you know a particle's position, the less precisely you can know its momentum. This is not a limit of our instruments but a property of nature.
- **Quantised energy:** electrons in atoms can only occupy certain energy levels. When they drop between levels they emit photons of exact colours, which is how we identify elements in distant stars.

## Why engineers care

Quantum theory is not only philosophy; it is the basis of modern electronics:

- **Semiconductors:** band theory, a quantum idea, explains why silicon can be tuned from insulator to conductor by doping, which makes diodes and transistors possible.
- **LEDs and lasers:** electrons dropping across an energy gap emit light of a precise colour. Lasers amplify this into a coherent beam used in fibre optics, barcode scanners and surgery.
- **Tunnelling:** particles can pass through barriers they classically could not cross. Flash memory writes data this way, and it limits how small transistors can shrink.

# Key points
- Special relativity: the speed of light is the same for all observers; moving clocks run slow.
- E = mc²: a small mass holds huge energy; fission and fusion release it.
- GPS corrects about 38 microseconds a day of relativistic clock drift.
- Photons carry energy E = hf; the photoelectric effect depends on frequency, not brightness.
- Quantum mechanics: superposition, uncertainty and quantised energy levels.
- Semiconductors, LEDs, lasers and flash memory all rely on quantum effects.
