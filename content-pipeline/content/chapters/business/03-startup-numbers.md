## Why founders must know the numbers

A great product can still sink a company if every sale loses money or the bank account runs dry. You do not need to be an accountant, but you must understand a handful of numbers well enough to spot danger early. Investors will ask about them in the first meeting.

## Revenue, costs and gross margin

- **Revenue** is the money earned from sales.
- **Cost of goods sold (COGS)** is the direct cost of delivering what you sold: materials, hosting per user, payment fees, delivery.
- **Gross profit** = revenue − COGS.
- **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue.

> Example: a solar lamp sells for 40 and costs 28 to make, ship and collect payment for. Gross profit = 12, gross margin = 12 ÷ 40 = 30%.

Gross margin shows how much of each sale is left to pay for everything else. Software often has margins above 70%, because another copy costs almost nothing. Hardware, retail and logistics businesses are often at 20–40%, so they need much larger volumes.

## Fixed costs, variable costs and break-even

**Variable costs** rise with each unit sold (materials, delivery). **Fixed costs** stay the same regardless of sales (rent, salaries, software subscriptions).

The **break-even point** is where total profit is zero:

> Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit)

If fixed costs are 6,000 a month and each lamp earns 12 after variable costs, you must sell 6,000 ÷ 12 = 500 lamps a month to break even. Every lamp after that adds 12 of profit. This is **operating leverage**: once fixed costs are covered, profits grow quickly.

## Unit economics: CAC and LTV

**Unit economics** asks whether a single customer is profitable.

- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** = total sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers won. If you spend 3,000 on ads and agents and win 100 customers, CAC = 30.
- **Lifetime value (LTV)** is the gross profit a customer brings over their whole relationship with you. For a subscription: LTV ≈ monthly gross profit per customer ÷ monthly churn rate.
- **Churn** is the share of customers who leave each period. A monthly churn of 5% means the average customer stays about 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 months.

> Example: a customer pays 10 a month at 70% gross margin (7 gross profit), and monthly churn is 5%. LTV ≈ 7 ÷ 0.05 = 140. With CAC = 30, LTV ÷ CAC ≈ 4.7.

A common rule of thumb is **LTV at least 3 × CAC**, with CAC recovered within about 12 months (the **payback period**). If LTV is below CAC, growing faster just loses money faster.

## Burn rate and runway

- **Gross burn** is total monthly spending; **net burn** is spending minus revenue, the cash you actually lose each month.
- **Runway** = cash in the bank ÷ net burn.

With 600,000 in the bank and a net burn of 50,000 a month, runway is 12 months. Raising money typically takes three to six months, so founders start fundraising with plenty of runway left. Paul Graham calls a startup **default alive** if, at current growth and costs, it will reach profitability before its cash runs out, and **default dead** if it won't.

## The three financial statements

Every company produces three core reports:

- **Income statement** (profit and loss): revenue minus expenses over a period gives **net profit**.
- **Balance sheet**: a snapshot of what the company owns (**assets**) and owes (**liabilities**) at one moment. The difference is **equity**: assets = liabilities + equity.
- **Cash flow statement**: actual cash moving in and out, from operations, investing and financing.

They tell different stories. A company can be **profitable but out of cash** if customers pay in 90 days while suppliers must be paid in 30, or if it buys lots of stock. Many businesses that fail were profitable on paper. Watch **cash flow** weekly, chase unpaid invoices, and negotiate payment terms.

## Metrics that can fool you

- **Vanity metrics:** total downloads and registered users always go up and hide whether people stay. Prefer active users and retention.
- **Revenue growth bought with discounts:** if customers leave when prices return to normal, you bought revenue, not demand.
- **Averages that hide cohorts:** look at each month's new customers (a **cohort**) separately to see whether retention is improving.

## Pricing is a lever

Raising prices by 10% with the same costs flows almost entirely to profit, while cutting costs by 10% is usually much harder. Price according to the **value** customers receive, not just your costs. Test prices, and remember that the right customers often care more about reliability than a small discount.

# Key points
- Gross margin = (revenue − COGS) ÷ revenue; it shows what each sale leaves to cover other costs.
- Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).
- CAC is the cost to win a customer; LTV is the gross profit they bring; aim for LTV ≥ 3 × CAC.
- Runway = cash ÷ net burn; start fundraising well before it runs out.
- Profit is not cash: the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement tell different stories.
- Beware vanity metrics; track retention by cohort.

# Quiz
Q: A product sells for 50 and costs 35 to produce and deliver. What is its gross margin?
- 15%
* 30%
- 35%
- 70%
> Gross profit is 15, and 15 ÷ 50 = 30%.

Q: Fixed costs are 8,000 a month; price is 20 and variable cost is 12 per unit. What is the break-even volume?
- 400 units
- 667 units
* 1,000 units
- 8,000 units
> Each unit contributes 20 − 12 = 8, and 8,000 ÷ 8 = 1,000 units.

Q: A startup has 300,000 in cash and a net burn of 25,000 a month. What is its runway?
- 6 months
* 12 months
- 25 months
- 3 months
> Runway = 300,000 ÷ 25,000 = 12 months.

Q: How can a profitable company run out of cash?
- It cannot; profit always means cash
* Customers pay slowly while costs and suppliers must be paid now
- Profit is only calculated once a year
- The balance sheet is always wrong
> Timing differences between earning revenue and receiving cash can empty the bank account.

Q: Monthly gross profit per customer is 6 and monthly churn is 3%. Roughly what is the LTV?
- 18
- 60
* 200
- 600
> LTV ≈ 6 ÷ 0.03 = 200.
