## Where VC money comes from

A **venture capital (VC) fund** is a pool of money managed by a VC firm. The firm's partners, the **general partners (GPs)**, choose the investments. The money mostly comes from **limited partners (LPs)**: pension funds, university endowments, insurance companies, development finance institutions and wealthy families.

A typical fund lasts about **ten years**: it invests in its first few years, helps companies grow, then returns money to LPs as companies are sold or listed. GPs are commonly paid "**2 and 20**": an annual management fee of about 2% of the fund, plus about 20% of the profits (**carried interest**).

## The power law

Most startups fail, and many of the rest return little. VC returns follow a **power law**: one outstanding company often returns more than all the others combined. So a VC must ask of every investment: **could this return the entire fund?**

This explains VC behaviour that otherwise seems strange. VCs prefer huge markets, accept high risk, push for fast growth, and pass on good businesses that are unlikely to become very large. A company that grows steadily into a profitable 5-million-dollar business can be a great outcome for its founders and a poor one for a VC fund.

## Funding rounds

Startups raise money in stages, each meant to reach milestones that justify a higher valuation next time:

- **Pre-seed:** founders, friends and family, angels and accelerators. Money to build the first version.
- **Seed:** angels and seed funds. Money to find product-market fit.
- **Series A:** the first big institutional round, for a company with clear traction that needs to scale.
- **Series B, C and later:** larger rounds to expand into new markets, products and teams.
- **Exit:** investors get their return when the company is **acquired** or lists on a stock exchange in an **initial public offering (IPO)**.

## Valuation and dilution

When an investor buys new shares, the key numbers are:

- **Pre-money valuation:** what the company is agreed to be worth before the new money.
- **Post-money valuation** = pre-money + new investment.
- **Investor's ownership** = investment ÷ post-money.

> Worked example: an investor puts in 2 million at an 8 million pre-money valuation. Post-money = 10 million, so the investor owns 2 ÷ 10 = 20%. A founder who owned 50% before now owns 50% × 0.8 = 40%.

That drop is **dilution**. It is not bad in itself. Right after the round, the founder's stake is worth the same on paper: 50% of 8 million and 40% of 10 million are both 4 million. Dilution pays off when the cash grows the company's value faster than your percentage shrinks: if the company later reaches 50 million, that 40% is worth 20 million. Founders track all of this in a **cap table**, a spreadsheet showing who owns what.

Companies also set aside an **option pool**, typically 10–20% of shares, to attract employees with stock options. Investors often require the pool to be created before their investment, so it dilutes only the founders.

## SAFEs and convertible notes

Agreeing a valuation for a very young company is hard, so early rounds often use instruments that convert into shares later:

- A **SAFE** (simple agreement for future equity), created by Y Combinator in 2013, gives the investor the right to shares in a future priced round. It usually has a **valuation cap** (a maximum valuation at which it converts) and sometimes a **discount**.
- A **convertible note** is similar but is technically a loan, with interest and a maturity date.

They are fast and cheap to arrange, but stacking many of them can cause surprise dilution when they all convert.

## The term sheet

A **term sheet** summarises the deal. Beyond valuation, founders should understand:

- **Liquidation preference:** in a sale, investors get their money back (usually 1×) before common shareholders. **Participating** preferences let them take their money back *and* share in the rest, which is much worse for founders.
- **Board composition:** who controls the board controls the company, including hiring and firing the CEO.
- **Protective provisions:** investor vetoes over major decisions such as new share issues or selling the company.
- **Pro-rata rights:** the right to invest in future rounds to keep their percentage.
- **Anti-dilution protection:** adjusts investor shares if a later round is priced lower (a **down round**).

A founder who focuses only on the highest valuation can end up with harsh terms that matter far more.

## Alternatives to venture capital

VC suits only a small fraction of businesses. Alternatives include:

- **Bootstrapping** from revenue, keeping full control.
- **Angel investors**, often more flexible than funds.
- **Grants and competitions**, common for African startups from foundations and development agencies.
- **Revenue-based financing**, repaid as a percentage of revenue.
- **Bank loans and asset finance**, for businesses with steady cash flow and collateral.

## The African picture

Venture investment in Africa grew rapidly from the mid-2010s, peaked in 2021–2022, then fell back as global interest rates rose. Most of the money has gone to fintech and to four markets: Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa and Egypt. Founders elsewhere, including in Zimbabwe, often incorporate a holding company abroad, commonly in Delaware or Mauritius, because many investors prefer familiar legal systems. Get legal and tax advice before doing this.

# Key points
- VC funds invest LPs' money, last about ten years, and are paid through fees and carried interest.
- Returns follow a power law, so VCs back companies that could return the whole fund.
- Post-money = pre-money + investment; investor ownership = investment ÷ post-money.
- Dilution is worthwhile only if the money grows the company's value faster.
- SAFEs and convertible notes delay valuation; watch valuation caps and stacked dilution.
- Liquidation preferences and board control can matter more than the headline valuation.
