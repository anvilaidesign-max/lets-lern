## A startup is built to grow

Every startup is a new business, but not every new business is a startup. A barbershop or a hardware shop can be an excellent business that grows steadily. A **startup** is designed to search for a business model that can grow **very fast**, usually by using technology so that serving the next thousand customers costs little extra.

That goal changes everything: how you fund it, who you hire, and how much risk you take. Most startups fail. The ones that succeed can become enormous: Stripe, Airbnb, Flutterwave.

## Start with a painful problem

The most common reason startups fail is building something nobody wants. So start with a **problem**, not a product:

- **Who** has the problem? Be specific: "small shop owners in Harare who sell on credit", not "everyone".
- **How painful** is it? Are they already spending time or money on a bad solution? That is the strongest signal.
- **Why now?** What has changed, such as cheaper smartphones, mobile money or new regulations, that makes a solution possible today?

Talk to potential customers before you build. Ask about their past behaviour ("How did you handle this last month?"), not hypothetical opinions ("Would you use an app that...?"). People are polite about ideas; their actions tell the truth.

## The lean startup

Eric Ries's book *The Lean Startup* (2011) popularised a method borrowed from science and from lean manufacturing. Treat your business plan as a set of **hypotheses**, and test them quickly:

- **Build** the smallest thing that tests your riskiest assumption.
- **Measure** what real users actually do.
- **Learn** whether the hypothesis was right.

Then decide to **persevere** or **pivot**. The aim is to learn as much as possible for as little time and money as possible, because a startup's real enemy is running out of runway before finding something that works.

## The minimum viable product

A **minimum viable product (MVP)** is the simplest version of the product that lets you learn. It can be surprisingly basic:

- A landing page describing the product, measuring how many people sign up.
- A **concierge MVP**: you do the service by hand, such as matching customers to plumbers over WhatsApp, before writing any code.
- A **Wizard of Oz MVP**: it looks automated to the user, but people do the work behind the scenes.

Dropbox famously tested demand with a short demo video before the product was finished; sign-ups for its waiting list jumped overnight. If you're not a little embarrassed by your first version, you probably launched too late.

## Product-market fit

**Product-market fit** means you have built something a clear market strongly wants. Before it, everything feels like pushing a boulder uphill. After it, customers pull the product out of your hands: usage grows by word of mouth, and people are upset when it's down.

Signals to watch:

- **Retention:** do users keep coming back weeks later? A flattening retention curve (a stable group that stays) is the single best sign.
- **Organic growth:** do new users arrive through referrals without paid advertising?
- **Willingness to pay**, and customers asking for more.

Before product-market fit, focus almost entirely on the product and a small group of users. After it, focus shifts to scaling: hiring, sales and raising money.

## Pivots

A **pivot** is a change in one fundamental part of the strategy while keeping what you have learned. Famous pivots:

- **Slack** began as an internal chat tool inside a company building an online game called *Glitch*. The game failed; the chat tool became Slack.
- **Instagram** started as *Burbn*, a check-in app with many features. The founders noticed people mainly used photo sharing and cut everything else.

A pivot is not failure; it is the lean loop working. But pivoting every month without learning anything is just thrashing.

## The founding team

Investors often say they back teams before ideas, because ideas change and teams execute. Strong founding teams:

- combine skills, typically someone who **builds** (technical) and someone who **sells**;
- have worked together before or know each other well;
- agree early and in writing on roles, ownership and **vesting**.

## Startups in Africa

African startups often solve problems that were solved long ago elsewhere: payments, logistics, credit, energy, health access. Mobile phones reached people before banks, roads and power grids did, which is why mobile money, pay-as-you-go solar and mobile health spread so quickly. Designing for low data costs, unreliable power, feature phones and cash-heavy customers is a real advantage for local founders.

# Key points
- A startup is a business designed to grow very fast, usually using technology.
- Start with a painful, specific problem, and learn from customers' past behaviour, not opinions.
- The lean startup loop: build the smallest test, measure real behaviour, learn, then persevere or pivot.
- An MVP exists to learn; it can be a landing page or a manual service.
- Retention is the best early signal of product-market fit.
- Pivots such as Slack and Instagram change strategy while keeping what was learned.
