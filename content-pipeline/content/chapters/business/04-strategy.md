## What strategy is

Strategy is the set of choices about **where to compete** and **how to win**, and just as importantly, what **not** to do. A strategy that tries to serve everyone with everything is not a strategy. Good strategy explains why customers will choose you over the alternatives, and why competitors can't simply copy you.

## Porter's five forces

In 1979, Harvard professor Michael Porter described five forces that decide how profitable an industry is:

- **Rivalry among existing competitors.** Many similar firms fighting on price destroy profits.
- **Threat of new entrants.** If starting up is cheap and easy, profits attract newcomers until they disappear.
- **Threat of substitutes.** Different products solving the same need: mobile money is a substitute for bank transfers, and video calls for business flights.
- **Bargaining power of suppliers.** A sole supplier of a key component can raise prices and take your margin.
- **Bargaining power of buyers.** Large customers, or customers who can switch easily, push prices down.

![The five forces: rivalry at the centre, pressed by new entrants, substitutes, suppliers and buyers.](five_forces)

Airlines face all five strongly and have historically struggled to make money. Software platforms with loyal users face weak forces and earn high margins. Before entering a market, ask which forces will squeeze you.

## Generic strategies

Porter also argued that firms win in one of a few basic ways:

- **Cost leadership:** be the cheapest producer, through scale, efficiency or a simpler product, and win on price.
- **Differentiation:** offer something customers value and will pay more for: quality, brand, design, service or features.
- **Focus:** apply either approach to a narrow segment, such as a specific region or customer type, better than broad competitors can.

The danger is being "**stuck in the middle**": neither cheapest nor clearly better.

## Moats

A **moat**, a term popularised by investor Warren Buffett, is a durable advantage that protects profits from competitors:

- **Network effects:** the product becomes more valuable as more people use it.
- **Switching costs:** leaving is painful because of data, integrations, retraining or contracts. Accounting software and banking relationships are examples.
- **Economies of scale:** unit costs fall with size, so big players can undercut newcomers.
- **Brand:** trust and recognition that let you charge more.
- **Intangible assets:** patents, licences and regulatory approvals.
- **Proprietary data or technology** that others can't easily replicate.

Startups rarely begin with a moat. They usually win at first through speed and focus, then build moats as they grow.

## Network effects in detail

With **direct** network effects, each new user adds value for every other user: WhatsApp is useless if none of your contacts use it. **Two-sided** (indirect) network effects link different groups: more riders attract more drivers on a ride-hailing app, which cuts waiting times and attracts more riders.

Mobile money is a powerful example. Each new EcoCash or M-Pesa user makes the network more useful for everyone they might pay, and more merchants accept it, which attracts more users. Network effects make markets tend towards one or two winners, which is why startups in these markets fight hard for early growth, and why regulators worry about dominance.

The hard part is the **cold start**: the first users get little value. Common tactics are to start in a small, dense community (one campus or one market), subsidise one side, or offer a useful single-player tool first.

## Go-to-market

How will customers find and buy your product?

- **Product-led growth:** users try it free and upgrade; it works best for simple, low-cost products.
- **Sales-led:** a sales team sells to businesses; it suits complex or expensive products.
- **Agent networks:** common in Africa for mobile money, insurance and solar, where trust and cash handling need a local person.
- **Partnerships:** distributing through a bank, telecom or retailer that already has the customers.

The best channel matches your price: a product earning 5 dollars a year can't afford a salesperson for each customer.

## Two quick planning tools

- **SWOT** lists internal **strengths** and **weaknesses** and external **opportunities** and **threats**. It is quick and useful for discussion, but only a starting point.
- The **business model canvas** describes a business on one page: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. It is excellent for spotting gaps and comparing options.

## Case study: Econet

In the 1990s, Strive Masiyiwa spent about five years in court fighting for the right to run a private mobile network in Zimbabwe. After winning, Econet Wireless grew quickly because mobile phones filled a huge gap left by limited fixed lines. It then used its network and customer base to launch **EcoCash** in 2011, adding financial services on top of telecoms. That is a classic strategy of building on an existing advantage, customers and agent networks, to enter a neighbouring market.

# Key points
- Strategy means choosing where to compete, how to win and what not to do.
- Porter's five forces: rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power and buyer power.
- Generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation or focus; avoid being stuck in the middle.
- Moats include network effects, switching costs, scale, brand, intangible assets and proprietary data.
- Network effects create winner-take-most markets but face a cold-start problem.
- Match the go-to-market channel to the product's price and complexity.

* Being neither the lowest-cost producer nor clearly differentiated
- Operating in the middle of a country
- Having medium-sized competitors
- Charging exactly the market price
> Firms without a clear cost or differentiation advantage tend to earn lower profits.

- A cheap advertising campaign
* Accounting software holding years of a company's records and integrations
- A new product launch

> Moving data, integrations and trained staff to a rival is costly, which keeps customers loyal.

- Servers are slow in winter
* Early users get little value because few others are on the network yet
- Customers refuse to pay

> Network value grows with users, so the first users need another reason to join.
