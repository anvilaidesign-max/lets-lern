## Before colonialism

The country takes its name from **Great Zimbabwe**, a stone city near present-day Masvingo, built mainly between the 11th and 15th centuries by ancestors of the Shona people. At its height it may have housed around 10,000 people, and it was the centre of a state that grew rich from cattle and from trading gold and ivory with the Indian Ocean coast. Its dry-stone walls, built without mortar, are among the largest ancient structures in sub-Saharan Africa. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and its famous soapstone birds appear on the national flag.

Later states included the **Mutapa** (Munhumutapa) kingdom in the north, which traded with Swahili and later Portuguese merchants, and the **Rozvi** state. In the 1830s and 1840s, **Mzilikazi** led the Ndebele north from present-day South Africa and founded a kingdom centred on **Bulawayo**, later ruled by his son **Lobengula**.

## Colonial conquest

In 1888 Lobengula signed the **Rudd Concession**, granting mining rights to agents of Cecil Rhodes. He believed he had granted limited rights; Rhodes's **British South Africa Company** used it to justify occupation. In 1890 the company's **Pioneer Column** raised the flag at what became **Salisbury** (now Harare). The Ndebele kingdom was defeated in 1893.

In 1896–97, Ndebele and Shona people rose against company rule in the **First Chimurenga** (Umvukela). Spirit mediums **Mbuya Nehanda** and **Sekuru Kaguvi** helped inspire the Shona uprising; both were captured and executed. Nehanda's reported words, that her bones would rise again, became a symbol of later resistance.

## Southern Rhodesia and UDI

In 1923 the territory became the self-governing British colony of **Southern Rhodesia**, run by and for a small white settler minority. Laws such as the **Land Apportionment Act** of 1930 reserved the best farmland for white settlers and pushed Africans onto crowded, poorer land, a grievance that shaped politics for the next century. From 1953 to 1963 it was part of the **Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland**, with present-day Zambia and Malawi.

As colonies across Africa gained independence, Britain refused to grant it without progress towards majority rule. On 11 November 1965, Prime Minister **Ian Smith** declared independence unilaterally, the **Unilateral Declaration of Independence (UDI)**. No country formally recognised Rhodesia, and the UN imposed sanctions.

## The liberation war

African nationalists, banned and jailed at home, turned to armed struggle. Two movements led the fight:

- **ZANU**, with its army **ZANLA**, later led by Robert Mugabe and based mainly in Mozambique.
- **ZAPU**, led by Joshua Nkomo, with its army **ZIPRA**, based mainly in Zambia.

The **Second Chimurenga** is often dated from the **Battle of Chinhoyi** on 28 April 1966 and intensified through the 1970s. Tens of thousands of people died. Under military and economic pressure, Smith's government accepted negotiations, leading to the **Lancaster House Agreement** in London in December 1979. It set up a ceasefire, a new constitution with some protections for the white minority and property, and elections.

## Independence

ZANU won the February 1980 elections, and on **18 April 1980** Zimbabwe became independent, with **Robert Mugabe** as Prime Minister and **Canaan Banana** as ceremonial President. The early years brought rapid expansion of schools and clinics, and literacy rose to among the highest in Africa.

They also brought **Gukurahundi**: from 1983 to 1987, the army's Fifth Brigade carried out killings in Matabeleland and the Midlands, with thousands of civilians killed, commonly estimated at around 20,000. The **Unity Accord** of 1987 merged ZANU and ZAPU into **ZANU-PF**. That same year the constitution was changed to create an **executive presidency**, and Mugabe became President.

## Crisis years

The 1990s brought an IMF-backed structural adjustment programme, job losses and rising discontent. In 1999 trade unionists and civil society formed the **Movement for Democratic Change (MDC)**, led by **Morgan Tsvangirai**, which became a strong opposition.

From 2000, the **fast-track land reform programme** took most commercial farms, largely owned by white farmers, for redistribution. It addressed a real historical injustice in land ownership, but it was carried out with violence and disorder, farm output collapsed, and many farm workers lost their jobs and homes. Together with sanctions, political conflict and heavy money printing, this helped drive the economy into decline, ending in the **hyperinflation** of 2007–2008.

After a disputed 2008 election marked by violence, SADC mediation led to a **Government of National Unity** (2009–2013), with Mugabe as President and Tsvangirai as Prime Minister. The US dollar replaced the Zimbabwe dollar, stabilising prices, and a new **Constitution** was approved by referendum in 2013.

## A new era?

In November 2017, after Mugabe dismissed his deputy **Emmerson Mnangagwa**, the military intervened. Facing impeachment, Mugabe resigned after 37 years in power, and Mnangagwa became President. He won the 2018 and 2023 elections, both disputed by the opposition, with observers raising concerns about the fairness of the process.

Zimbabwe's politics today revolves around questions that run through its whole history: land, the economy and currency, the relationship between the ruling party, the state and the military, and how to build institutions that protect every citizen's rights.

# Key points
- Great Zimbabwe (11th–15th centuries) was the capital of a rich Shona state and gives the country its name.
- The British South Africa Company colonised the territory from 1890; the First Chimurenga followed in 1896–97.
- Ian Smith's UDI in 1965 led to the liberation war fought by ZANLA and ZIPRA.
- The Lancaster House Agreement (1979) led to independence on 18 April 1980.
- Gukurahundi, the 1987 Unity Accord, land reform, hyperinflation and the 2013 Constitution shaped the modern state.
- Mugabe resigned in November 2017 after a military intervention; Mnangagwa succeeded him.
