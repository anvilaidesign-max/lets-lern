## Free and fair elections

An election is only meaningful if it is both **free** (people can take part without fear) and **fair** (everyone competes on equal terms). Election observers, such as those from the African Union, SADC and civil society groups, assess the whole process, not just polling day:

- an **accurate, accessible voters' roll** that parties can inspect;
- an **independent electoral commission**;
- freedom to campaign, assemble and speak, without violence or intimidation;
- **fair access to media**, especially state broadcasters;
- a **secret ballot** and transparent counting, with results posted at each polling station;
- fair rules on **campaign finance** and use of state resources; and
- a credible way to **challenge results** in court.

## Electoral systems

The **electoral system**, the rules that turn votes into seats, can change the result as much as the votes themselves.

**First-past-the-post (FPTP)** divides the country into constituencies. In each, the candidate with the most votes wins, even without a majority. It is simple and links each MP to a local area, but:

- a party can win most seats with well under half the votes;
- votes for losing candidates count for nothing, which hurts smaller parties spread thinly across the country;
- it tends to produce two dominant parties, a pattern known as **Duverger's law**.

**Proportional representation (PR)** gives parties seats in proportion to their share of the vote, usually from **party lists**. It is fairer to smaller parties and gives more accurate representation, but it often produces coalition governments and weakens the link between voters and a local representative, and party leaders gain power over who gets on the list.

**Mixed systems** combine both. Germany and New Zealand elect some members by constituency and top up with list seats to make the overall result proportional.

## Two-round systems for presidents

Many countries elect presidents by **absolute majority**: if no candidate wins more than half the votes in the first round, the top two meet in a **run-off**. This ensures the winner has majority support. Zimbabwe uses this system: a run-off was held in 2008 after no candidate won a majority in the first round.

## Zimbabwe's system

Under the 2013 Constitution, Zimbabweans vote in **harmonised elections** every five years for the President, Parliament and local councils on the same day:

- The **President** needs more than half of the valid votes, otherwise there is a run-off.
- The **National Assembly** has **210 constituency seats** elected by FPTP, plus **60 seats reserved for women** allocated by proportional representation across the provinces. A **youth quota** of 10 more seats was added from the 2023 election.
- The **Senate** has 80 members, most elected by proportional representation from party lists in each province, plus traditional chiefs and representatives of people with disabilities.

The **Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC)** runs elections. Disputes over presidential results go directly to the **Constitutional Court**, which must decide within 14 days.

## Parties and ideologies

**Political parties** gather people with similar views to compete for power, choose candidates, develop policies and hold government to account in opposition.

The **left–right spectrum** is a rough guide to ideas about the economy:

- The **left** tends to favour more state involvement: public services, redistribution through taxes and stronger worker protections.
- The **right** tends to favour free markets, lower taxes, private enterprise and traditional values.

Real parties mix positions, and in many African countries party loyalty is shaped as much by history, region, ethnicity and the liberation struggle as by economic ideology.

## Accountability between elections

Voting every five years is not enough to keep power in check. Between elections, accountability comes from:

- **Parliament:** questions to ministers, committee inquiries and control of the budget.
- **Courts:** judicial review of government decisions.
- **Independent commissions:** auditors, anti-corruption and human rights bodies.
- **The media:** investigative journalism exposes wrongdoing.
- **Civil society:** unions, churches, professional associations, students and non-governmental organisations.
- **Citizens:** petitions to Parliament, peaceful protest and contacting representatives.

## Information and misinformation

Voters need accurate information. Social media spreads news quickly, but also **misinformation** (false information shared by mistake) and **disinformation** (lies spread deliberately). Before sharing political news, check the source, look for the same story from reliable outlets, check dates and photos, and be most suspicious of claims that make you angry.

# Key points
- Free and fair elections need an accurate roll, an independent commission, freedom to campaign, media access, a secret ballot and dispute resolution.
- First-past-the-post is simple and local but can give majorities on a minority of votes.
- Proportional representation mirrors votes more accurately but often leads to coalitions.
- Zimbabwe elects its President by absolute majority with a run-off, and uses FPTP plus PR quota seats for Parliament.
- Accountability between elections comes from Parliament, courts, commissions, the media and civil society.
