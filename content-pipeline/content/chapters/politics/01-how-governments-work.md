## State, nation and government

These words are often mixed up, but they mean different things:

- A **state** is a political organisation with a permanent population, a defined territory, a government and the capacity to deal with other states. Zimbabwe, Kenya and France are states.
- A **nation** is a people who share an identity, such as language, history or culture. Some nations have no state of their own; many states contain several nations.
- The **government** is the group of people and institutions running the state at a given time. Governments change; the state continues.

The sociologist Max Weber defined the state as the organisation that successfully claims a **monopoly on the legitimate use of force** within its territory. Only the police, army and courts may lawfully use force; everyone else must go to them.

## Sovereignty and legitimacy

**Sovereignty** means supreme authority within a territory, free from outside control. It is the basic rule of international politics: states are equal in law and should not interfere in each other's internal affairs, though human rights law and UN action complicate this.

**Legitimacy** is the belief that those in power have the right to rule. Governments that are seen as legitimate need less force to govern. Weber described three sources of legitimacy:

- **Traditional:** "it has always been so", as in monarchies and chieftaincies.
- **Charismatic:** devotion to an extraordinary leader, common among liberation heroes.
- **Legal-rational:** authority comes from following fair, known rules, such as elections and constitutions. This is the basis of modern democracies.

## Democracy and its alternatives

**Democracy** means rule by the people. In **direct democracy**, citizens vote on laws themselves, as in ancient Athens or modern referendums. Most democracies are **representative**: citizens elect people to make laws for them.

Elections alone do not make a democracy. Political scientists look for:

- free and fair elections with real competition;
- freedom of expression, assembly and association;
- a free press and access to independent information;
- the **rule of law** and independent courts;
- protection of minority rights; and
- civilian control of the military.

**Authoritarian** regimes concentrate power, restrict opposition and limit freedoms, even if they hold elections. Many countries are **hybrid regimes** in between, with elections that are held but not fully fair, or courts that are only partly independent.

## Presidential and parliamentary systems

- In a **parliamentary system** (the UK, South Africa, India), voters elect parliament, and the government is formed by whoever commands a majority there. The **prime minister** can be removed by a vote of no confidence. The head of state (a monarch or ceremonial president) is separate. South Africa is unusual: its parliament elects a president who is both head of state and head of government.
- In a **presidential system** (the USA, Kenya, Brazil), voters elect the president separately for a fixed term. The president is both head of state and head of government, and cannot usually be removed by parliament except through impeachment.
- **Semi-presidential systems** (France) combine an elected president with a prime minister accountable to parliament.

Zimbabwe moved from a parliamentary system with a prime minister in 1980 to an **executive presidency** in 1987. Under the 2013 Constitution, the President is directly elected and leads the government, and Parliament has two houses: the National Assembly and the Senate.

## Unitary and federal states

- In a **unitary state**, the central government holds sovereign power and may delegate some of it to regions. Zimbabwe is unitary, though its Constitution provides for **devolution** to provincial and metropolitan councils and local authorities.
- In a **federal state** (Nigeria, Ethiopia, the USA, India), the constitution divides power between the central government and states or regions, each with its own guaranteed powers.

Federalism can help large, diverse countries hold together by giving regions self-government, but it can also entrench divisions.

## Checks and balances

The French thinker Montesquieu argued in 1748 that liberty is safest when power is split between the **legislature** (makes laws), the **executive** (carries them out) and the **judiciary** (judges disputes under the law), so that no single person or group holds it all. Each branch can limit the others:

- Parliament passes budgets, questions ministers and can impeach a president.
- The executive proposes laws and appoints officials, often including judges.
- Courts can declare laws and government actions unconstitutional.

![Each branch has tools to check the other two, so no branch holds all the power.](separation_powers)

Independent institutions add further checks: electoral commissions, auditors-general, anti-corruption commissions, human rights commissions and a free press. Zimbabwe's Constitution creates several of these as independent commissions.

## Why this matters

The structure of government decides how easy it is to remove bad leaders, how well minorities are protected, and whether policy is stable enough for people to invest and plan. Studying it helps you judge news and political promises, and understand what citizens can actually do to hold power to account.

# Key points
- A state has territory, population, government and sovereignty; a nation is a people with a shared identity.
- Weber: the state holds the monopoly on legitimate force; legitimacy can be traditional, charismatic or legal-rational.
- Democracy requires more than elections: rights, free media, rule of law and independent courts.
- Parliamentary systems make the government answerable to parliament; presidential systems elect the executive separately.
- Zimbabwe is a unitary state with an executive president and a two-house Parliament.
- Separation of powers and independent institutions check the abuse of power.
