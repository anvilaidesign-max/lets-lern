## What law is for

Law is the set of rules a society enforces through its institutions. It does three big jobs: it keeps order (criminal law), it lets people make reliable deals and settle disputes (civil law), and it limits the power of the state itself (constitutional law).

The ideal behind all three is the **rule of law**: rules are public, clear and applied equally, including to the government, by independent courts. When the rule of law is strong, people can plan, invest and challenge abuse of power. When it is weak, outcomes depend on who you know.

## Where law comes from

A lawyer answering a question in Zimbabwe checks several **sources of law**, in order of authority:

- **The Constitution.** The supreme law. Anything inconsistent with it is invalid.
- **Legislation.** Acts of Parliament, plus **statutory instruments**: detailed regulations made by ministers or other bodies under powers an Act gives them.
- **Common law.** In Zimbabwe this is **Roman-Dutch law**: the law applied in the Cape Colony on 10 June 1891, carried north by colonial proclamation and developed by the courts since.
- **Case law.** Judges' decisions interpret all of the above, and under the doctrine of **precedent** lower courts follow the reasoning of higher ones.
- **Customary law.** The traditional law of communities, mainly in family, inheritance and communal land matters.
- **International law.** Treaties Zimbabwe has ratified and customary international law; the Constitution tells courts to consider international law when interpreting rights.

## Roman-Dutch law, English influence

Roman-Dutch law grew from ancient Roman law as developed in the Netherlands, and was taken to the Cape by Dutch settlers. When the British took over the Cape, they kept it, but English law heavily influenced procedure, evidence and commercial law. The result, shared with South Africa, Namibia, Botswana, Lesotho and Eswatini, is a **mixed legal system**.

This has practical effects. Zimbabweans speak of a **delict** where English lawyers say tort, and a contract does not need "consideration" (something given in return) to be valid, unlike in English law. South African court decisions are often cited in Zimbabwe as persuasive authority, because the two systems are so close.

## The 2013 Constitution

Zimbabwe's current Constitution was approved by referendum in March 2013 and signed into law in May 2013, replacing the Lancaster House Constitution of 1979. It has since been amended twice, in 2017 and 2021. Key features:

- **Supremacy** (section 2): any law, practice, custom or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution is invalid to that extent.
- **The Declaration of Rights** (Chapter 4): life, dignity, equality, freedom of expression and assembly, fair trial, property, education, health care and more. Most rights can be limited only by a law that is fair, reasonable and necessary in a democratic society.
- **Separation of powers**: Parliament makes laws, the President and Cabinet run government, and the courts interpret the law.
- **Devolution** to provincial and local tiers of government.

Anyone whose rights are infringed can approach a court, and the Constitutional Court can declare laws and conduct invalid.

## The courts

- **The Constitutional Court** is the highest court on constitutional matters.
- **The Supreme Court** is the final court of appeal in all other matters.
- **The High Court** can hear any civil or criminal case, handles the most serious crimes and big civil claims, hears appeals from magistrates and reviews government decisions.
- **Specialised courts** sit at a similar level: the **Labour Court** for employment disputes and the **Administrative Court** for appeals against decisions on matters like tax and licensing.
- **Magistrates' courts** handle the great majority of criminal and civil cases.
- **Local courts** presided over by chiefs and headmen deal with customary law disputes.

## Criminal and civil cases

The same event can lead to two different cases. If a drunk driver injures you, the **state** prosecutes the driver in a criminal case, where a conviction can mean a fine or prison. Separately, **you** can sue the driver in a civil case for your medical bills and lost income.

The two use different **standards of proof**. Criminal guilt must be proved **beyond reasonable doubt**; a civil claim succeeds on a **balance of probabilities**, meaning more likely than not.

## Reading the law yourself

Legislation is cited by name and chapter, such as the Labour Act [Chapter 28:01]. Always check you are reading the latest version, including amendments and statutory instruments. For anything important, such as a contract, a dispute or an arrest, get advice from a registered legal practitioner. Legal aid is available for people who cannot afford one.

# Key points
- The Constitution is Zimbabwe's supreme law; anything inconsistent with it is invalid.
- Other sources: Acts and statutory instruments, Roman-Dutch common law, case law, customary law and international law.
- Zimbabwe has a mixed system: Roman-Dutch law with strong English influence, shared with South Africa.
- The Constitutional Court decides constitutional questions; the Supreme Court is the final court of appeal otherwise.
- Criminal cases need proof beyond reasonable doubt; civil cases a balance of probabilities.
