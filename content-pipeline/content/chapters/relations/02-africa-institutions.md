## From the OAU to the African Union

On **25 May 1963**, 32 independent African states founded the **Organisation of African Unity (OAU)** in Addis Ababa, which is why 25 May is celebrated as **Africa Day**. The OAU's priorities were ending colonialism and apartheid, defending sovereignty and promoting unity. It supported liberation movements across southern Africa, including Zimbabwe's.

But its strict principle of **non-interference** meant it stayed silent while some member governments committed terrible abuses. In 2002 it was replaced by the **African Union (AU)**, launched in Durban and headquartered in Addis Ababa. The AU now has **55 member states**, covering the whole continent.

The key change was a move from non-interference to **non-indifference**. The AU's Constitutive Act gives it the right to intervene in a member state in grave circumstances: **war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity**. It also rejects governments that take power unconstitutionally, and it has suspended several countries after coups.

## How the AU works

- **The Assembly** of heads of state and government is the supreme body.
- **The AU Commission**, the secretariat, is led by a chairperson.
- **The Peace and Security Council** handles conflicts and can deploy peace-support missions.
- **The Pan-African Parliament** is currently advisory.
- **Agenda 2063** is the AU's long-term plan for an integrated, prosperous and peaceful Africa.

In 2023 the AU became a permanent member of the **G20**, giving Africa a seat at the table of the world's largest economies.

## Regional economic communities

The AU recognises eight **regional economic communities (RECs)** as its building blocks, including **ECOWAS** in West Africa, the **EAC** in East Africa, **ECCAS** in Central Africa, **IGAD** in the Horn of Africa, **COMESA** across East and Southern Africa, and **SADC**. Many countries belong to more than one, which creates overlapping rules, sometimes called the "spaghetti bowl" problem.

## SADC

The **Southern African Development Community** grew out of the Southern African Development Coordination Conference (SADCC), formed in 1980 by the **Frontline States** to reduce economic dependence on apartheid South Africa. It became SADC in 1992 and has its headquarters in **Gaborone**, Botswana. It has 16 members, including Zimbabwe, South Africa, Zambia, Mozambique, Botswana, Namibia, Angola, Malawi, Tanzania and the DRC.

SADC works on trade (a free trade area launched in 2008), infrastructure such as the **Southern African Power Pool**, which lets countries trade electricity, and peace and security. It has been deeply involved in Zimbabwe: SADC mediation, led by South Africa's President Thabo Mbeki, produced the 2008 **Global Political Agreement** that created the Government of National Unity. SADC also sent a military mission to fight insurgents in northern Mozambique from 2021.

## The African Continental Free Trade Area

The **AfCFTA** agreement was signed in **Kigali in 2018**, and trading under it officially began on **1 January 2021**. It aims to create a single market for goods and services across the continent, with around **1.4 billion people**. By the number of participating countries, it is the largest free trade area in the world. Its secretariat is in Accra, Ghana.

Why it matters: only around 15% of African countries' trade is with each other, compared with well over half within Europe. Much of Africa exports raw materials to other continents and imports finished goods. A bigger regional market could help African manufacturers reach the scale needed to compete, and add value locally, for example turning Zimbabwe's lithium into battery components for regional industries.

The obstacles are real: poor roads and railways, slow borders, non-tariff barriers, different standards and currencies, and fears that stronger economies will dominate. Tariff cuts are being phased in over years.

## Africa and the wider world

Africa's international relationships are becoming more diverse:

- **China** is Africa's largest trading partner and a major lender and builder of infrastructure through the **Forum on China–Africa Cooperation (FOCAC)** and the **Belt and Road Initiative**. Supporters point to new roads, railways, ports and power stations; critics point to debt burdens, opaque contracts and imported labour.
- **Europe** remains a major trading partner, aid donor and investor, and the destination of much African migration.
- **The United States** trades with Africa through the **African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA)**, which gives eligible countries duty-free access for many goods.
- **The BRICS** group (Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa, which joined in 2010) expanded in 2024 to include **Egypt and Ethiopia**, among others. Many African governments see it as a way to gain influence in a world order they feel was designed without them.
- **The Gulf states, Turkey, Russia and India** have all expanded their presence.

Many African states practise **non-alignment**, avoiding firm sides in rivalries between major powers and working with whoever offers the best deal.

# Key points
- The OAU (1963) fought colonialism; the AU (2002, 55 members) replaced non-interference with non-indifference.
- The AU can intervene in cases of genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity, and suspends coup governments.
- SADC (1992, Gaborone, 16 members) grew from the Frontline States' SADCC and mediated Zimbabwe's 2008 crisis.
- The AfCFTA (signed 2018, trading since 2021) aims to build one market of about 1.4 billion people.
- Intra-African trade is low, at around 15% of the continent's trade.
- Africa's partnerships are diversifying: China, Europe, the US, the BRICS and others.
