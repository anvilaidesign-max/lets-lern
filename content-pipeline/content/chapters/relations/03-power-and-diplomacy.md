## Why theories help

News reports tell you what happened. **Theories** help explain why, and what might happen next. Each major theory is a lens that highlights some causes and hides others. Good analysts use several.

## Realism: power and survival

**Realism** starts from the anarchic international system: there is no world government, so states must ensure their own survival. Key ideas:

- States are the main actors, and they pursue their **national interest**, above all security.
- **Power**, especially military and economic power, is what ultimately counts.
- States watch the **balance of power** and join together against any state that threatens to dominate.
- Moral arguments and international institutions matter little when vital interests are at stake.

Classical realists such as Hans Morgenthau emphasised human nature's drive for power. **Neorealists** such as Kenneth Waltz argued that the structure of the system itself, anarchy plus the distribution of power, forces states to compete, whatever their leaders are like.

Realism explains arms races, alliances and why great powers often ignore rules when their interests demand it. It is weaker at explaining lasting cooperation, such as the European Union or the AfCFTA.

## Liberalism: cooperation is possible

**Liberalism** accepts that anarchy exists but argues that states can cooperate for mutual gain:

- **Economic interdependence:** countries that trade heavily have more to lose from war.
- **International institutions** such as the UN, WTO and AU reduce uncertainty, set rules and make cheating easier to spot.
- **Democratic peace theory:** established democracies have very rarely, if ever, fought wars against each other.
- Non-state actors, such as companies, NGOs and international organisations, also shape world politics.

Liberalism explains the growth of trade agreements, international law and regional integration. Critics say it underestimates how quickly cooperation collapses when security is at stake.

## Constructivism: ideas make the world

**Constructivism** argues that world politics is shaped by **ideas, identities and norms**, not just material power. In Alexander Wendt's famous phrase, "anarchy is what states make of it". In Wendt's own example, 500 British nuclear weapons worried the United States less than 5 North Korean ones, because of shared identity and history, not the numbers.

Norms can change behaviour over time: colonialism, apartheid and slavery went from accepted to condemned. Pan-Africanism, a shared identity rooted in the anti-colonial struggle, helps explain African solidarity at the UN and the AU. Constructivism is good at explaining change, but it is harder to use for precise predictions.

## Hard and soft power

Power is the ability to get others to do what you want. The political scientist **Joseph Nye** distinguished:

- **Hard power:** coercion through military force and economic pressure, such as sanctions, or inducement through payments.
- **Soft power:** attraction through culture, values, education and policies others see as legitimate. Hollywood films, Korean pop music, scholarships to study abroad and admired institutions all generate soft power.
- **Smart power:** combining both effectively.

Countries invest heavily in soft power: China through Confucius Institutes and scholarships, the US and UK through universities and media, and African countries through music, film, sport and diplomacy.

## Diplomacy

**Diplomacy** is the management of relations between states through negotiation. Its tools include embassies, summits, envoys, treaties and mediation. Under the **Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations** (1961), diplomats enjoy **immunity** from arrest and their embassies are inviolable, so that communication between states can continue even during crises.

Mediation by third parties is common in African conflicts: SADC in Zimbabwe (2008), the AU in Sudan and Ethiopia, and regional leaders in many other disputes. Successful mediation usually needs leverage over the parties, patience, and a deal that gives each side something it can present as a win.

## Sanctions

**Sanctions** restrict trade, finance or travel to pressure a government or individuals. **Comprehensive** sanctions target a whole economy; **targeted** or "smart" sanctions freeze the assets of, and ban travel for, named people and companies. Evidence suggests sanctions rarely change a determined government's core behaviour, and broad sanctions can hurt ordinary citizens more than leaders.

Zimbabwe has lived with this debate for decades. From the early 2000s, the United States and the European Union imposed measures, mostly targeted at individuals and companies linked to the government, while the US **Zimbabwe Democracy and Economic Recovery Act** (2001) restricted support from international lenders. The government and SADC called them illegal sanctions that damaged the economy; Western governments said they were aimed at human rights abuses and corruption. In March 2024 the US ended its Zimbabwe-specific sanctions programme, while placing some individuals under its global anti-corruption sanctions instead.

## Game theory: why cooperation is hard

The **prisoner's dilemma** shows why rational actors may fail to cooperate even when cooperation would benefit both. Two countries could both cut military spending and gain; but each fears being left weaker if the other cheats, so both keep spending.

When the game is **repeated**, cooperation becomes easier. Strategies like **tit-for-tat** (cooperate first, then copy the other side's last move) do well, because the "shadow of the future" gives each side a reason to keep its reputation. International institutions help by making the game repeated, transparent and rule-based.

## Analysing a world event

Faced with a crisis, ask:

- **Realist:** who gains or loses power and security?
- **Liberal:** what economic ties, institutions and domestic politics are involved?
- **Constructivist:** what identities, histories and norms shape how each side sees the other?

Then ask who the actors are beyond states: companies, armed groups, diasporas and international organisations. Good analysis combines lenses and checks them against evidence.

# Key points
- Realism: states pursue power and security under anarchy; the balance of power matters most.
- Liberalism: trade, institutions and democracy make cooperation possible.
- Constructivism: ideas, identities and norms shape interests ("anarchy is what states make of it").
- Hard power coerces; soft power attracts (Joseph Nye).
- Diplomacy relies on negotiation and immunity; sanctions are often less effective than intended.
- Repeated interaction and institutions help overcome the prisoner's dilemma.
