## A world of sovereign states

International relations studies how states, organisations, companies and people interact across borders: war and peace, trade, diplomacy, migration, climate and human rights.

The modern system is often traced to the **Peace of Westphalia** of 1648, which ended decades of religious war in Europe. It established principles that became global rules:

- **Sovereignty:** each state has supreme authority over its own territory.
- **Non-intervention:** states should not interfere in each other's internal affairs.
- **Legal equality:** big and small states are equal in law.

European colonialism denied these principles to most of the world. After 1945, **decolonisation** expanded the system from about 50 states to nearly 200, most of them in Africa and Asia.

## Anarchy: no world government

Inside a country, a government makes and enforces laws. Between countries there is no world government, no global police and no court with compulsory power over everyone. Scholars call this **anarchy**, meaning not chaos, but the absence of a higher authority.

Anarchy explains a lot. States must look after their own security, which can lead to the **security dilemma**: when one state builds up its army for defence, neighbours feel threatened and arm too, so everyone ends up less safe. It also explains why cooperation, even when it benefits everyone, is hard to guarantee: agreements depend on trust, reputation and self-interest.

## The United Nations

The **United Nations** was founded in 1945, after the Second World War, to maintain peace and security, develop friendly relations among nations and promote human rights and development. It began with 51 members and now has **193**. Its **Charter** bans the threat or use of force against other states, except in **self-defence** or when authorised by the Security Council.

Its main parts:

- **General Assembly:** all members, one vote each. It debates global issues and passes resolutions, which are mostly recommendations, not binding law.
- **Security Council:** responsible for peace and security. Its decisions are **binding** on all members, and it can authorise sanctions and military action.
- **Secretariat:** the UN's staff, led by the **Secretary-General**.
- **International Court of Justice (ICJ):** in The Hague, it settles legal disputes between states that accept its jurisdiction.
- **Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)** and specialised agencies and funds such as the **WHO**, **UNICEF**, the **World Food Programme** and **UNHCR**, the refugee agency.

## The Security Council and the veto

The Security Council has 15 members. Ten are elected for two-year terms, including three seats for Africa. Five are **permanent members**, the victors of 1945: **China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States**. Each permanent member has a **veto**: any one of them can block a resolution.

The veto kept the great powers inside the UN, but it also means the Council is often paralysed when a permanent member or its ally is involved. African states, through the AU's **Ezulwini Consensus** (2005), demand at least two permanent seats for Africa with full veto rights, arguing that a continent of over 50 countries, and the subject of much of the Council's work, cannot be left without a permanent voice.

## Peacekeeping

UN **peacekeeping** missions, made up of troops, police and civilians contributed by member states, monitor ceasefires, protect civilians and support peace processes. African countries are among the largest contributors of peacekeepers, and many missions have operated in Africa, including in the DRC, South Sudan and Mali. Peacekeeping works best when there is a peace to keep; it struggles when fighting continues.

## International courts

- The **ICJ** judges **states**, for example in border disputes, and only with their consent.
- The **International Criminal Court (ICC)**, created by the **Rome Statute** and operating since 2002, tries **individuals** for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression, when national courts are unwilling or unable to act.

The ICC has been criticised in Africa because most of its early cases involved Africans, while powerful states such as the United States, China and Russia are not members. Supporters reply that several African cases were referred by African governments themselves. The debate over fairness in international justice continues.

## Governing the world economy

In 1944, at **Bretton Woods** in the United States, the Allies created institutions to rebuild and stabilise the world economy:

- The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** lends to countries in balance-of-payments crises, usually with policy conditions attached.
- The **World Bank** lends and gives grants for development projects.
- Trade rules later came under the **World Trade Organization (WTO)**, founded in 1995, which settles trade disputes between members.

These institutions matter enormously for developing countries, and they are criticised for giving rich countries most of the voting power and for harsh conditions, such as the structural adjustment programmes of the 1980s and 1990s.

# Key points
- The Westphalian system rests on sovereignty, non-intervention and legal equality of states.
- Anarchy means no world government; it creates the security dilemma and makes cooperation harder.
- The UN (1945, 193 members) bans the use of force except self-defence or Security Council authorisation.
- The five permanent members of the Security Council each hold a veto; Africa seeks permanent seats.
- The ICJ settles disputes between states; the ICC tries individuals for the gravest crimes.
- The IMF, World Bank and WTO shape the world economy.
