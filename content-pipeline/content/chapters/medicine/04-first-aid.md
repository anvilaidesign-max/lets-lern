## Why everyone should know this

In an emergency, the minutes before trained help arrives often decide the outcome. First aid is not about being a doctor; it is about keeping someone alive and safe until professionals take over. This chapter gives general knowledge only. A practical course, such as those run by the Red Cross, teaches the hands-on skills properly and is worth doing.

## The first steps: DRSABC

Many first aid courses teach a sequence like **DRSABC**:

- **Danger:** check for danger to yourself and others first (traffic, fire, live electricity). You cannot help if you become a casualty.
- **Response:** speak loudly and tap the shoulders. Is the person awake and responding?
- **Send for help:** call the emergency services or get someone else to call. Put the phone on speaker so you can follow instructions.
- **Airway:** tilt the head back gently and lift the chin to open the airway.
- **Breathing:** look, listen and feel for normal breathing for up to 10 seconds. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing.
- **CPR:** if the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR.

## CPR for adults

In cardiac arrest the heart stops pumping effectively, and every minute without CPR sharply reduces the chance of survival. Chest compressions keep blood moving to the brain.

- Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest, the other hand on top.
- Keep your arms straight and push hard and fast: about **5 to 6 cm deep** at **100 to 120 compressions per minute**, letting the chest rise fully between compressions.
- If trained and willing, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions (30:2). If not, **hands-only CPR** (continuous compressions) is far better than nothing.
- If an **AED** (automated external defibrillator) is available, switch it on and follow its spoken instructions. It will only shock if the heart rhythm needs it.

Keep going until help arrives, the person starts breathing normally, or you are too exhausted to continue.

## The recovery position

If someone is unresponsive but **breathing normally**, roll them onto their side with the top leg bent to stop them rolling, and tilt the head back so vomit or saliva can drain rather than block the airway. Keep checking their breathing.

## Choking

If an adult can cough, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot cough, speak or breathe, give up to 5 firm **back blows** between the shoulder blades, then up to 5 **abdominal thrusts** (standing behind them, fist above the navel, pulling sharply inwards and upwards). Alternate until the object clears, and call for help. If they become unresponsive, start CPR.

## Severe bleeding

Apply firm, direct pressure to the wound with a clean cloth or dressing, and keep pressing. If blood soaks through, add more on top rather than removing the first layer. Keep the person lying down and warm. Wear gloves or use a plastic bag as a barrier if possible, to protect yourself from blood-borne infections.

## Burns

Cool the burn under cool, clean running water for **at least 20 minutes**, which reduces damage even after the burning has stopped. Remove rings and tight items before swelling starts, but do not pull away clothing stuck to the burn. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean non-fluffy cloth. Do not apply butter, toothpaste or ice. Large burns, deep burns, and burns to the face, hands or genitals need medical care.

## Electric shock

**Do not touch the person** while they may still be in contact with the source. Switch off the power at the switch or breaker first. For high-voltage lines, keep well away and call the utility and emergency services, because current can arc through the ground. Once safe, check breathing and start CPR if needed. Anyone who has had a significant shock should be medically checked, because electricity can disturb the heart's rhythm and cause internal burns that are not visible. This is essential knowledge for anyone working with electrical installations.

## Dehydration and diarrhoea

Diarrhoea, especially cholera, kills mainly through dehydration. **Oral rehydration solution (ORS)** saves lives: sachets are mixed with clean water. If sachets are not available, a common home recipe is 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of clean water. Keep drinking small, frequent sips, continue feeding, and seek care for danger signs such as very little urine, sunken eyes, confusion, or diarrhoea in a small child.

# Key points
- Check for danger first; then response, send for help, airway, breathing, CPR.
- CPR: push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, 5-6 cm deep, 100-120 per minute.
- Hands-only CPR is much better than nothing; use an AED as soon as one arrives.
- Choking: back blows then abdominal thrusts, alternating.
- Burns: cool running water for at least 20 minutes; no butter or ice.
- Electric shock: switch off the power before touching the person.
