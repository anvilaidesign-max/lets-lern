## The germs

Infectious diseases are caused by microorganisms (pathogens):

- **Bacteria** are single-celled living organisms. Most are harmless or helpful (your gut holds trillions), but some cause cholera, typhoid, tuberculosis and pneumonia. Antibiotics can kill them.
- **Viruses** are far smaller and not truly alive on their own: they hijack your cells to copy themselves. Influenza, measles, HIV and COVID-19 are viral. Antibiotics do nothing against them; some have antiviral drugs, and many are best prevented by vaccines.
- **Parasites** include the malaria parasite *Plasmodium* and worms such as bilharzia (schistosomiasis), common in freshwater in parts of Africa.
- **Fungi** cause infections such as thrush and ringworm.

They spread through the air (TB, flu), contaminated water and food (cholera, typhoid), body fluids (HIV, hepatitis B), and insect bites (malaria).

## The immune system

Your body defends itself in layers.

**Barriers:** skin, mucus, stomach acid and tears stop most germs getting in.

**Innate immunity** responds within hours to anything foreign. White blood cells called phagocytes engulf invaders, and inflammation (redness, heat, swelling) brings more defenders to the site. Fever raises body temperature, which slows many pathogens.

**Adaptive immunity** is slower at first but precise and has memory:

- **B cells** make **antibodies**, proteins that lock onto a specific pathogen and mark it for destruction.
- **T cells** kill infected cells and coordinate the response. **CD4 T cells** are the coordinators, and they are exactly what HIV destroys.
- **Memory cells** remain after an infection, so a second encounter with the same germ is defeated quickly, often before you feel ill. This is immunity.

## How vaccines work

A **vaccine** trains adaptive immunity without the danger of the disease. It presents a harmless version or part of the pathogen: an inactivated germ, a weakened live germ, a protein piece, or, in mRNA vaccines, instructions for your cells to make a harmless protein piece. The immune system responds and forms memory cells, so it is ready if the real pathogen arrives.

When enough people are immune, a disease cannot spread easily, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns and people with weak immunity. This is **herd immunity**. Vaccination eradicated smallpox, declared in 1980, and has brought polio close to eradication.

## Malaria

Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium* parasites transmitted by the bites of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes, mostly between dusk and dawn. Symptoms such as fever, chills and headache usually start 10 to 15 days after the bite. The WHO African Region carries the great majority of the world's cases and deaths, and young children are most at risk. Prevention: insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying and, for travellers, preventive medicine. Fever in a malaria area needs a prompt test, because early treatment with artemisinin-based combination therapy saves lives. Malaria vaccines (RTS,S and R21) are now being rolled out for children.

## HIV

HIV attacks CD4 T cells. Without treatment it slowly weakens immunity until **AIDS** develops, when infections the body would normally control become deadly. HIV spreads through unprotected sex, shared needles, blood, and from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It does not spread through hugging, sharing food or mosquito bites.

**Antiretroviral therapy (ART)** does not cure HIV but controls it. A person who takes ART daily can live a long, healthy life, and when the virus is suppressed to undetectable levels they **do not transmit HIV sexually** ("undetectable = untransmittable", U=U). Testing, condoms, PrEP (preventive medicine for people at risk) and treating pregnant mothers all prevent new infections.

## Tuberculosis

TB is a bacterial infection, usually of the lungs, spread through the air when someone with active TB coughs. Classic signs are a cough lasting more than two weeks, fever, night sweats and weight loss. TB is curable with a strict course of several antibiotics taken for months; stopping early breeds drug-resistant TB. People living with HIV are at much higher risk, so the two are screened for together.

# Key points
- Bacteria can be treated with antibiotics; viruses cannot.
- Innate immunity responds fast to anything foreign; adaptive immunity is specific and has memory.
- B cells make antibodies; T cells kill infected cells; HIV destroys CD4 T cells.
- Vaccines create immune memory safely; herd immunity protects the vulnerable.
- Malaria spreads by female Anopheles mosquito bites; test fevers early.
- ART controls HIV; an undetectable viral load means HIV is not transmitted sexually (U=U).
