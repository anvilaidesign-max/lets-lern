## Two questions

**Pharmacology** studies how drugs act on the body and how the body acts on drugs. Pharmacologists split this into two questions:

- **Pharmacodynamics:** what does the drug do to the body?
- **Pharmacokinetics:** what does the body do to the drug?

## What drugs do: targets

Most drugs work by binding to specific molecules, often **receptors** on cells or **enzymes**:

- An **agonist** activates a receptor, mimicking a natural signal. Salbutamol in asthma inhalers activates receptors that relax the airways.
- An **antagonist** blocks a receptor so the natural signal cannot act. Beta blockers block adrenaline's effect on the heart, lowering heart rate and blood pressure.
- **Enzyme inhibitors** block an enzyme. Aspirin and ibuprofen block enzymes that make inflammatory chemicals, reducing pain, fever and swelling.

Antibiotics target structures that bacteria have and human cells don't: penicillin, for example, stops bacteria building their cell walls.

## What the body does: ADME

Pharmacokinetics follows a drug through four stages, often summarised as **ADME**:

- **Absorption:** getting into the blood, from the gut, skin, lungs or an injection. Some drugs are destroyed in the stomach or liver before they reach the circulation, which is why insulin is injected rather than swallowed.
- **Distribution:** travelling around the body to the tissues.
- **Metabolism:** mostly in the liver, enzymes chemically change the drug, usually to make it easier to remove.
- **Excretion:** removal, mainly by the kidneys into urine.

This is why liver and kidney disease can change the dose a person needs.

## Dose and half-life

The **half-life** is the time it takes for the drug's concentration in the blood to fall by half. After about four to five half-lives, a drug is mostly gone. Half-life decides how often you take a medicine: a drug with a half-life of 6 hours might be taken every 6 to 8 hours, while one lasting days might be taken weekly.

With regular doses, levels build up until intake balances removal, reaching a **steady state** after about four to five half-lives. That is why some medicines take days to reach their full effect, and why doctors sometimes start with a larger **loading dose**.

## The therapeutic window

Every drug has a range where it helps without causing unacceptable harm: the **therapeutic window**. Below it, it does not work; above it, it becomes toxic. "The dose makes the poison": paracetamol is safe at normal doses, but an overdose can destroy the liver. Some drugs, such as the blood thinner warfarin, have narrow windows and need regular blood tests.

## Side effects and interactions

No drug is perfectly selective, so most have **side effects**, from mild (drowsiness from some antihistamines) to serious. **Allergic reactions** are different: the immune system overreacts, and severe cases (**anaphylaxis**) are emergencies treated with adrenaline.

**Interactions** happen when one substance changes another's effect. Some drugs speed up or slow down the liver enzymes that break other drugs down; certain antibiotics, some HIV and TB medicines, alcohol and even herbal remedies can raise or lower drug levels. Always tell a health worker everything you take.

## Antibiotic resistance

Bacteria multiply fast and mutate. When antibiotics are used, the few bacteria that happen to resist survive and multiply. Overuse (antibiotics for viral colds), stopping courses early, poor-quality medicines and heavy use in farming all accelerate this. The WHO lists **antimicrobial resistance** among the top global public health threats, because infections that were easily cured become hard or impossible to treat. Use antibiotics only when prescribed, and take the full course exactly as directed.

## How new medicines are proven

New drugs go through **clinical trials** before approval:

- **Phase 1:** a small group, often healthy volunteers, to test safety and dosing.
- **Phase 2:** a few hundred patients, to test whether it works and refine the dose.
- **Phase 3:** hundreds to thousands of patients, often in a **randomised controlled trial** comparing the drug with a placebo or current treatment, with neither patients nor doctors knowing who gets what (**double-blind**).
- **Phase 4:** monitoring after approval, to catch rare side effects.

Randomisation and blinding matter because belief alone can make people feel better (the **placebo effect**), and doctors' expectations can bias results.

# Key points
- Pharmacodynamics: what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics: what the body does to the drug.
- Agonists activate receptors; antagonists block them; many drugs inhibit enzymes.
- ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism (mostly liver), excretion (mostly kidneys).
- Half-life sets dosing frequency; steady state takes about four to five half-lives.
- The therapeutic window lies between ineffective and toxic doses.
- Antibiotic resistance grows with misuse; finish prescribed courses.

> Antagonists occupy the receptor without activating it, blocking the body's own signal.

- 50 mg
- 25 mg
* 12.5 mg
- 0 mg
> 12 hours is three half-lives: 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 mg.

Q: Which organ does most drug metabolism?
- The heart
* The liver
- The lungs
- The skin
> Liver enzymes chemically transform most drugs; the kidneys do most excretion.

Q: What is the therapeutic window?
* The dose range where a drug works without unacceptable toxicity
- The time of day a drug must be taken
- The first phase of a clinical trial
- The period before a drug expires
> Too little does nothing; too much is harmful. The window lies between.

Q: Why are clinical trials randomised and double-blind?
- To make trials faster
- To save money
* To prevent expectations and bias from distorting the results
- To hide the results from regulators
> Random allocation and blinding stop the placebo effect and biased judgement from creating false results.
