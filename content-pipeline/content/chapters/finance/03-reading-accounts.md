## Why accounts matter

Whether you are investing, lending, joining a company or running one, the financial statements tell you whether it makes money, whether it can pay its bills, and how it is funded. Every company produces three core statements. Each answers a different question, and together they are hard to fake completely.

## The income statement

The **income statement** (profit and loss, or P&L) covers a period, such as a year:

- **Revenue** (sales)
- − **Cost of sales** = **gross profit**
- − **Operating expenses** (salaries, rent, marketing, research) = **operating profit**
- − **Interest** and **tax** = **net profit** (the "bottom line")

Profit follows **accrual accounting**: revenue is recorded when earned, not when cash arrives, and expenses when incurred, not when paid.

**Depreciation** spreads the cost of long-lasting assets over their useful life. A 100,000 machine expected to last 10 years might be charged at 10,000 a year. It reduces profit without any cash leaving that year, because the cash left when the machine was bought.

Analysts often quote **EBITDA**: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It is a rough measure of operating cash generation, but it ignores the real cost of replacing equipment, so treat it with caution.

## The balance sheet

The **balance sheet** is a snapshot on one date:

> Assets = Liabilities + Equity

- **Assets** are what the company owns or controls: cash, money owed by customers (**receivables**), inventory, equipment, buildings, patents.
- **Liabilities** are what it owes: suppliers (**payables**), loans, taxes.
- **Equity** is what belongs to the owners: money they invested plus profits kept in the business (**retained earnings**).

It always balances because every cent the company holds came either from lenders or from owners. **Current** assets and liabilities are those due within a year; they matter for short-term survival.

## The cash flow statement

The **cash flow statement** tracks real cash in three groups:

- **Operating activities:** cash from the day-to-day business. It starts from net profit, adds back non-cash costs like depreciation, and adjusts for changes in receivables, inventory and payables.
- **Investing activities:** buying or selling equipment, property or other businesses.
- **Financing activities:** borrowing, repaying loans, issuing shares and paying dividends.

A healthy mature company generates strong operating cash flow. **Free cash flow** = operating cash flow − capital expenditure: cash available to repay debt, pay dividends or grow.

## How the three connect

- Net profit from the income statement adds to **retained earnings** on the balance sheet.
- The cash flow statement explains why the **cash** line on the balance sheet changed.
- Buying a machine is investing cash outflow; the machine appears as an asset; its depreciation hits the income statement each year.

Warning signs appear when they disagree. Rising profits with persistently negative operating cash flow can mean customers are not paying, inventory is piling up, or profits are being flattered.

## Profitability ratios

- **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue
- **Operating margin** = operating profit ÷ revenue
- **Net margin** = net profit ÷ revenue
- **Return on equity (ROE)** = net profit ÷ equity: how much profit the owners' money earns. An ROE of 15% means each dollar of equity earned 15 cents.

## Safety ratios

- **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Below 1 means short-term debts exceed short-term resources, a liquidity warning.
- **Debt-to-equity** = total debt ÷ equity. Higher leverage magnifies returns in good years and losses in bad ones.
- **Interest cover** = operating profit ÷ interest expense. Below about 2 or 3, a bad year could make interest hard to pay.

## Valuation ratios

- **Price-to-earnings (P/E)** = share price ÷ earnings per share.
- **Dividend yield** = annual dividend per share ÷ share price.
- **Price-to-book** = market value ÷ book equity.

Ratios mean little alone. Compare them with the **same company over time** and with **similar companies**. A 5% net margin is excellent for a supermarket and poor for a software company.

## Worked example

> A company has revenue of 2,000,000, cost of sales of 1,200,000, operating expenses of 500,000, interest of 50,000 and tax of 60,000. Gross profit = 800,000 (40% margin). Operating profit = 300,000 (15%). Net profit = 300,000 − 50,000 − 60,000 = 190,000 (9.5%). With equity of 1,000,000, ROE = 19%. Interest cover = 300,000 ÷ 50,000 = 6: comfortable.

Always read the **notes** to the accounts and the **auditor's report**. A "qualified" or "adverse" audit opinion, or a sudden change of auditor, deserves attention.

# Key points
- The income statement shows profit over a period; the balance sheet a snapshot; the cash flow statement real cash movements.
- Assets = liabilities + equity, always.
- Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over its life and is a non-cash expense.
- Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditure.
- Key ratios: margins and ROE (profitability), current ratio, debt-to-equity and interest cover (safety), P/E (value).
- Compare ratios over time and with similar companies; read the notes and the audit report.

# Quiz
Q: A company's assets are 5 million and liabilities are 3 million. What is its equity?
- 8 million
* 2 million
- 3 million
- 5 million
> Equity = assets − liabilities = 5 − 3 = 2 million.

Q: A 60,000 machine lasts 5 years with no resale value. Using straight-line depreciation, what is the yearly charge?
- 6,000
* 12,000
- 60,000
- 30,000
> 60,000 ÷ 5 = 12,000 a year, charged against profit with no cash leaving in those years.

Q: Current assets are 400,000 and current liabilities are 500,000. What does the current ratio suggest?
- The company is very safe
* Short-term debts exceed short-term resources: a liquidity warning
- The company has no debt
- Profits are high
> The current ratio is 0.8, below 1, so it may struggle to pay bills due within a year.

Q: Where would a bank loan received this year appear in the cash flow statement?
- Operating activities
- Investing activities
* Financing activities
- It would not appear
> Borrowing and repaying loans, issuing shares and paying dividends are financing activities.

Q: Net profit is 150,000 and equity is 1,000,000. What is the return on equity?
- 1.5%
* 15%
- 66%
- 150%
> ROE = 150,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 15%.
