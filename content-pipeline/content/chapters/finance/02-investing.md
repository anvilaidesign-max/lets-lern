## Saving versus investing

**Saving** keeps money safe and available: a bank account or cash for emergencies. **Investing** puts money to work in assets expected to grow, accepting that their value will go up and down. Before investing, most planners suggest paying off expensive debt and building an **emergency fund** of a few months' expenses, so you are never forced to sell investments at a bad time.

## Shares: owning part of a company

A **share** (or stock) is a small piece of ownership in a company. Shareholders earn money in two ways:

- **Dividends:** part of the profits paid out in cash.
- **Capital gains:** the share price rising as the company grows and earns more.

Share prices move every day with news, interest rates and investor mood, but over long periods they tend to follow the company's **earnings**. A common valuation measure is the **price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio**: share price ÷ earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means investors pay 20 dollars for each dollar of yearly profit, which only makes sense if they expect profits to grow.

## Bonds: lending with an IOU

A **bond** is a loan to a government or company. You pay, say, 1,000; the issuer pays you fixed **coupons** (interest) and returns the 1,000 at **maturity**. Bonds are usually steadier than shares, but not risk-free:

- **Default risk:** the issuer may not pay. Higher-risk issuers must offer higher interest.
- **Interest rate risk:** when market interest rates **rise**, existing bonds paying lower fixed coupons become less attractive, so their **prices fall**. When rates fall, bond prices rise.
- **Inflation risk:** fixed payments lose value if inflation rises.

The **yield** is the return you get at the current price. Price and yield always move in opposite directions.

## Funds and index investing

A **fund** (unit trust, mutual fund or ETF) pools money from many investors and buys a wide range of assets. You get **diversification** with a small amount of money.

- **Active funds** pay managers to pick investments they think will beat the market. They charge higher fees, and research over decades shows most fail to beat the market after fees.
- **Index funds** simply buy every company in an index, such as the S&P 500 (500 large US companies), at very low cost. John Bogle launched the first index fund for ordinary investors at Vanguard in 1976, and index investing is now one of the most popular strategies in the world.

Fees matter more than they look: an extra 1.5% a year can consume a large part of a lifetime's returns, because fees compound too.

## Risk and return

There is no free lunch: assets with **higher expected returns** come with **higher risk**, meaning bigger swings and a greater chance of loss. Roughly from safest to riskiest: cash, government bonds, corporate bonds, property, diversified shares, individual shares, startups and speculative assets.

Your **time horizon** decides how much risk makes sense. Money needed next year should not be in shares, which can fall 30–50% in a crash. Money not needed for 20 years can ride out crashes, and historically diversified share portfolios have recovered and grown over such periods, though past performance is never a guarantee.

## Diversification

Holding one company's shares exposes you to that company's specific disasters: fraud, a failed product, a lost licence. Spread across many companies, industries and countries, those individual shocks largely cancel out. That is **diversification**, often called the only free lunch in finance, because it cuts risk without cutting expected return.

What diversification cannot remove is **market risk**, the risk that everything falls together in a recession. That remaining risk is what investors are paid to bear.

## Investing in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has two stock exchanges:

- The **Zimbabwe Stock Exchange (ZSE)** in Harare, with roots going back to 1896 and among Africa's oldest, trading in local currency.
- The **Victoria Falls Stock Exchange (VFEX)**, launched in 2020, trading in **US dollars**, which appeals to investors worried about currency risk.

Other options include treasury bills, money market funds, property and, through licensed brokers, offshore funds. Currency risk matters: an investment's return in local currency means little if the currency itself falls faster.

## How to spot a scam

Investment fraud thrives where people want high returns and trust is personal. Warning signs:

- **Guaranteed high returns** with "no risk", such as 10% a month.
- Returns paid from **new investors' money** (a **Ponzi scheme**) or rewards for recruiting others (a pyramid scheme).
- Pressure to **decide fast**, secrecy, or complicated stories about forex bots, crypto arbitrage or gold trading.
- The seller is **not licensed** by the securities regulator.

If it sounds too good to be true, it almost certainly is. Real investments with higher returns always carry real risk.

# Key points
- Shares are part-ownership earning dividends and capital gains; bonds are loans paying fixed coupons.
- When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall; price and yield move in opposite directions.
- Low-cost index funds beat most active funds after fees over long periods.
- Higher expected return means higher risk; match risk to your time horizon.
- Diversification removes company-specific risk but not market risk.
- Guaranteed high returns and payouts from new investors' money are the hallmarks of scams.

- It rises
* It falls
- It stays exactly the same
- It doubles
> Its fixed coupon is now less attractive than new bonds, so its price falls until its yield matches the market.

Q: A share costs 50 and the company earns 2.50 per share per year. What is its P/E ratio?
- 2.5
- 12.5
* 20
- 125
> P/E = 50 ÷ 2.50 = 20.

Q: What risk does diversification NOT remove?
- The risk of one company committing fraud
- The risk of one factory burning down
* Market risk: the whole market falling together
- The risk of one product failing
> Company-specific risks cancel out across many holdings, but a broad downturn affects them all.

Q: Why do index funds often beat actively managed funds over time?
* Their very low fees, while most active managers fail to beat the market after fees
- They only buy winning companies
- They are guaranteed by governments
- They avoid all market crashes
> Fees compound over time, and consistently beating the market is rare.

Q: A scheme promises 15% a month, guaranteed, and pays early members from new members' deposits. What is it?
- A bond fund
- A diversified index fund
* A Ponzi scheme
- A treasury bill
> Paying old investors with new investors' money is a Ponzi scheme; it collapses when new money slows.
